Chapter 1b — Neoclassical Growth Model

The Ramsey Model

Endogenous saving, CRRA utility, the Hamiltonian, and optimal consumption dynamics.

Motivation

Why an Endogenous Saving Rate?

In the Solow model, the saving rate $s$ is assumed to be exogenous and constant: households mechanically save a fixed fraction $s$ of their income regardless of interest rates, wages, or future expectations.

The Ramsey Departure:
  • There is no imposed constant saving rate $s$.
  • Households choose their consumption path $c(t)$ optimally over time.
  • Saving becomes endogenous: it is determined residually through the household's consumption decision.

To model forward-looking households making intertemporal choices, the Ramsey framework introduces two essential preference parameters:

1. Pure rate of time preference: $\rho > 0$

Measures impatience. A higher $\rho$ means households value present consumption relatively more compared with future consumption. Future utility is discounted at rate $\rho$.

2. Curvature parameter of utility: $\varepsilon > 0$

Governs the concavity of the utility function. The intertemporal elasticity of substitution is: $$\text{IES} = \frac{1}{\varepsilon}$$

Intuition on $\varepsilon$ and the Intertemporal Elasticity of Substitution ($\text{IES} = 1/\varepsilon$):

• High $\varepsilon$ (low $\text{IES}$): The household strongly dislikes fluctuations in living standards across time. It is less willing to substitute consumption across periods and insists on a very smooth consumption profile.
• Low $\varepsilon$ (high $\text{IES}$): The household is more willing to shift consumption between periods in response to economic incentives (such as a high return to capital).

Part 1 / 4

Household Instantaneous Utility Function $u(c)$

We consider a representative household that derives satisfaction from consumption per effective worker:

$$c = \frac{C}{AL}$$

The CRRA (Constant Relative Risk Aversion) Utility Function

In the Ramsey model, the instantaneous utility function takes the standard CRRA functional form:

$$\boxed{u(c) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{c^{1-\varepsilon} - 1}{1-\varepsilon} & \text{for } \varepsilon \neq 1 \\[10pt] \ln(c) & \text{for } \varepsilon = 1 \end{cases}}$$
The Logarithmic Case as a Limit: When $\varepsilon \to 1$, applying L’Hôpital’s rule to $\dfrac{c^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon}$ with respect to $\varepsilon$ gives: $$\lim_{\varepsilon \to 1} \frac{c^{1-\varepsilon} - 1}{1-\varepsilon} = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 1} \frac{\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}(c^{1-\varepsilon}-1)}{\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}(1-\varepsilon)} = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 1} \frac{-c^{1-\varepsilon}\ln(c)}{-1} = \ln(c)$$ Hence, logarithmic utility $u(c) = \ln(c)$ is the continuous limiting special case when $\varepsilon = 1$.

Explicit Derivation of Derivatives

1
Case $\varepsilon \neq 1$ — First Derivative (Marginal Utility): Differentiating $u(c) = \dfrac{c^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon}$ with respect to $c$: $$u'(c) = \frac{d}{dc}\left[\frac{c^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon}\right] = \frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot (1-\varepsilon)c^{(1-\varepsilon)-1} = c^{-\varepsilon}$$
$$\boxed{u'(c) = c^{-\varepsilon}}$$
Since $c > 0$ and $\varepsilon > 0$, we have $\mathbf{u'(c) > 0}$: marginal utility is strictly positive (more consumption increases utility).
2
Case $\varepsilon \neq 1$ — Second Derivative (Curvature): Differentiating $u'(c) = c^{-\varepsilon}$ with respect to $c$: $$u''(c) = \frac{d}{dc}\left[c^{-\varepsilon}\right] = -\varepsilon c^{-\varepsilon - 1}$$
$$\boxed{u''(c) = -\varepsilon c^{-(\varepsilon+1)}}$$
Since $\varepsilon > 0$ and $c > 0$, we have $\mathbf{u''(c) < 0}$: marginal utility is strictly decreasing. This concavity implies that households have a preference for smoother consumption paths rather than volatile spikes.
3
Special Case $\varepsilon = 1$ (Logarithmic Utility): When $u(c) = \ln(c)$: $$u'(c) = \frac{1}{c} = c^{-1} > 0$$ $$u''(c) = -\frac{1}{c^2} = -c^{-2} < 0$$ This is exactly identical to evaluating $c^{-\varepsilon}$ and $-\varepsilon c^{-\varepsilon-1}$ at $\varepsilon = 1$.

Standard Neoclassical Characteristics Satisfied

$u'(c) > 0$

Positive marginal utility: More consumption always yields higher satisfaction.

$u''(c) < 0$

Decreasing marginal utility: Each additional unit of consumption yields less extra utility than the previous one.

$\lim_{c \to 0} u'(c) = \infty$

Inada condition at 0: $\lim_{c \to 0} c^{-\varepsilon} = \infty$. The first drop of consumption is infinitely valuable, so households will never choose $c = 0$.

$\lim_{c \to \infty} u'(c) = 0$

Inada condition at $\infty$: $\lim_{c \to \infty} c^{-\varepsilon} = 0$. As consumption approaches infinity, additional satisfaction approaches zero.

The CRRA instantaneous utility function $u(c)$ is strictly increasing ($u'(c) > 0$) and strictly concave ($u''(c) < 0$).
Part 2 / 4

Intertemporal Utility Function $W$

Households do not simply maximise utility in a single period: they are forward-looking and evaluate an entire trajectory of consumption $\{c(t)\}_{t \ge 0}$ from the present into the indefinite future:

$$W = \int_0^\infty u(c(t))\,e^{-\rho t}\,dt$$

Understanding Every Component of the Integral

$t = 0$: The present moment / initial date at which the optimisation decision takes place.
$t \to \infty$: The infinite horizon. Important: $+ \infty$ does not literally mean until an individual dies. The Ramsey model uses an infinite horizon interpreted as an infinitely lived representative household or dynasty where parents care altruistically about their descendants.
Entire path $\{c(t)\}$: The household chooses a complete time path of consumption per effective worker $c(t)$, balancing current consumption against future living standards.
$e^{-\rho t}$: The discount factor. Because $\rho > 0$, $e^{-\rho t}$ declines exponentially over time: utility gained in the distant future is discounted more heavily.
$\rho > 0$: The pure rate of time preference (psychological impatience).

Substituting the Explicit CRRA Utility Function into $W$

Substituting the explicit CRRA form gives the actual intertemporal objective function used in the dynamic problem:

$$\boxed{W = \int_0^\infty \left[\frac{c(t)^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon}\right] e^{-\rho t}\,dt \qquad (\text{for } \varepsilon \neq 1)}$$
Special Case ($\varepsilon = 1$ — Logarithmic Utility):
$$W = \int_0^\infty \ln(c(t))\,e^{-\rho t}\,dt$$
Part 3 / 4

The Economy: Production & Capital Accumulation

The technology and demographic setting of the Ramsey model are identical to the Solow model with technological progress:

Effective labour: $AL$ with growth rate $g_A + n$
Technology growth: $g_A \equiv \dfrac{1}{A}\dfrac{dA}{dt}$
Population growth: $n \equiv \dfrac{1}{L}\dfrac{dL}{dt}$
Depreciation rate: $\delta > 0$
Capital per effective worker: $k = \dfrac{K}{AL}$
Consumption per effective worker: $c = \dfrac{C}{AL}$

Production per Effective Worker

$$Y = (AL)^{1-\alpha}K^\alpha \implies y = \frac{Y}{AL} = \left(\frac{K}{AL}\right)^\alpha = k^\alpha$$

The Ramsey Resource Constraint: Gross Investment vs. Break-Even Investment

Since output per effective worker is $k(t)^\alpha$ and households consume $c(t)$, what is left is channelled into gross investment:

$$\textbf{GROSS INVESTMENT: } \quad i(t) = k(t)^\alpha - c(t)$$

To maintain capital per effective worker $k(t)$ constant, the economy requires break-even investment:

$$\textbf{BREAK-EVEN INVESTMENT: } \quad (n + \delta + g_A)k(t)$$
• $\delta k(t)$: physical depreciation (capital wearing out)
• $n k(t)$: population dilution (spreading capital over more workers as $L$ grows)
• $g_A k(t)$: technological dilution (each worker becomes more productive as $A$ grows, so more capital is needed to keep $K/(AL)$ constant)

Therefore, net capital accumulation is simply:

$$\text{Net capital accumulation} = \text{Gross investment} - \text{Break-even investment}$$
$$\frac{dk}{dt} = \left[k(t)^\alpha - c(t)\right] - (n + \delta + g_A)k(t)$$

Rearranging gives the central equation of capital accumulation:

$$\boxed{\frac{dk}{dt} = k(t)^\alpha - c(t) - (n + \delta + g_A)k(t)}$$
1
Aggregate accumulation equation: Total output is split between consumption $C$ and gross investment $I$: $Y = C + I$. Net aggregate capital accumulation equals gross investment minus depreciation: $$\frac{dK(t)}{dt} = I - \delta K = Y - C - \delta K$$
2
Differentiating $k = \dfrac{K}{AL}$ with respect to time using the quotient rule: $$\frac{dk(t)}{dt} = \frac{\frac{dK(t)}{dt}(AL) - K\frac{d(A(t)L(t))}{dt}}{(AL)^2} = \frac{1}{AL}\frac{dK(t)}{dt} - \frac{K}{AL}\left(\frac{1}{AL}\frac{d(A(t)L(t))}{dt}\right)$$ Since $\dfrac{1}{AL}\dfrac{d(A(t)L(t))}{dt} = \dfrac{1}{A}\dfrac{dA(t)}{dt} + \dfrac{1}{L}\dfrac{dL(t)}{dt} = g_A + n$: $$\frac{dk(t)}{dt} = \frac{1}{AL}\frac{dK(t)}{dt} - (n + g_A)k$$
3
Substitute $\dfrac{dK(t)}{dt} = Y - C - \delta K$: $$\frac{dk(t)}{dt} = \frac{Y - C - \delta K}{AL} - (n + g_A)k = \frac{Y}{AL} - \frac{C}{AL} - \delta k - (n + g_A)k$$ Since $\dfrac{Y}{AL} = k^\alpha$ and $\dfrac{C}{AL} = c$: $$\boxed{\frac{dk(t)}{dt} = k^\alpha - c - (n + \delta + g_A)k}$$
Framework

Dynamic Optimisation: Control Variable vs. State Variable

Before solving the dynamic problem, we must clearly distinguish the two economic variables that govern the household's environment:

Variable Type Symbol Economic Meaning & Dynamic Nature
CONTROL VARIABLE $$c(t)$$ Chosen directly and instantaneously:

At any point in time $t$, the household can freely decide how much to consume right now. It can jump discontinuously if economic conditions or expectations change.

STATE VARIABLE $$k(t)$$ Inherited and predetermined:

The capital stock is inherited from previous investment decisions ($k(0)$ is historically given). It cannot jump instantaneously; it changes smoothly over time according to the capital accumulation differential equation: $$\frac{dk}{dt} = k(t)^\alpha - c(t) - (n + \delta + g_A)k(t)$$

Key Takeaway: The household controls its path of consumption $c(t)$ (the control variable), which in turn determines the rate of change of the economy's capital stock $k(t)$ (the state variable).
Part 4 / 4

Solving the Ramsey Model (1/3) — The Hamiltonian & FOCs

The dynamic optimisation problem for the household (with $\varepsilon \neq 1$) is:

$$\max_{\{c\}} \quad \int_0^\infty \left[\frac{c^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon}\right] e^{-\rho t}\,dt \qquad \text{subject to: } \frac{dk(t)}{dt} = k^\alpha - c - (n + \delta + g_A)k$$

The Current-Value Hamiltonian

We set up the Hamiltonian by adding the instantaneous utility and the product of the multiplier $\lambda$ with the capital accumulation rate $\dfrac{dk(t)}{dt}$:

$$\boxed{\mathcal{H} = \frac{c^{1-\varepsilon}-1}{1-\varepsilon} + \lambda\left[k^\alpha - c - (n + \delta + g_A)k\right]}$$

• Notice that the expression inside the brackets is exactly $\mathbf{\dfrac{dk(t)}{dt}}$.
• $\lambda$ is the multiplier / shadow value of capital: it measures the marginal value of having an additional unit of capital per effective worker today for future utility.

Two Types of First-Order Conditions

1
TYPE 1 — First-Order Condition on the CONTROL VARIABLE $c$:

Since $c$ is chosen freely at every instant, we set the derivative of $\mathcal{H}$ with respect to $c$ to zero:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{H}}{\partial c} = 0$$

Differentiating explicitly:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{H}}{\partial c} = c^{-\varepsilon} - \lambda = 0$$
$$\boxed{\lambda = c^{-\varepsilon}}$$

Economic Interpretation: The marginal utility of consumption $c^{-\varepsilon}$ must equal the shadow value $\lambda$ of capital. If consumption gave more utility than saving, the household would consume more; if capital had a higher shadow value, it would consume less.

2
TYPE 2 — Dynamic Condition on the STATE VARIABLE $k$:

⚠️ Do NOT set $\dfrac{\partial \mathcal{H}}{\partial k} = 0$!

Because $k$ is a state variable (its level is predetermined and evolves over time), its optimality condition is dynamic and describes how the shadow value $\lambda$ changes over time:

$$\frac{d\lambda(t)}{dt} = \rho\lambda - \frac{\partial \mathcal{H}}{\partial k}$$

First, compute the partial derivative of $\mathcal{H}$ with respect to $k$:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{H}}{\partial k} = \lambda\left[\alpha k^{\alpha-1} - (n + \delta + g_A)\right]$$

Substituting into the dynamic state-variable condition gives:

$$\frac{d\lambda(t)}{dt} = \rho\lambda - \lambda\left[\alpha k^{\alpha-1} - (n + \delta + g_A)\right]$$

or equivalently written as:

$$\boxed{\rho\lambda = \lambda\left[\alpha k^{\alpha-1} - (n + \delta + g_A)\right] + \frac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}}$$

Economic Interpretation: The required rate of return on saving ($\rho\lambda$) must equal the economic dividend from capital ($\lambda \times \text{net marginal product}$) plus the capital gain or loss on the shadow price ($\dfrac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}$).

3
Transversality Condition (Terminal Condition):
$$\boxed{\lim_{t \to \infty} e^{-\rho t}\,\lambda\,k = 0}$$

Economic Interpretation: The discounted shadow value of leftover capital cannot remain strictly positive forever. Households must not over-accumulate capital endlessly and leave valuable resources unconsumed as time goes to infinity.

Part 2 / 3

Solving the Ramsey Model (2/3) — The Ramsey Euler Equation

By combining the control-variable condition $\lambda = c^{-\varepsilon}$ and the state-variable condition, we derive the Ramsey Euler Equation governing optimal consumption growth.

$$\boxed{g_c \equiv \frac{1}{c}\frac{dc(t)}{dt} = \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\alpha k^{\alpha-1} - \rho - n - \delta - g_A\right]}$$
1
General derivation: From the control condition, $\lambda = u'(c)$. Differentiating both sides with respect to time $t$: $$\frac{d\lambda(t)}{dt} = u''(c)\frac{dc(t)}{dt}$$ Substitute this into the state-variable equation $\rho\lambda = \lambda\left[f'(k) - (n + \delta + g_A)\right] + \dfrac{d\lambda(t)}{dt}$: $$\rho\lambda = \lambda\left[f'(k) - (n + \delta + g_A)\right] + u''(c)\frac{dc(t)}{dt}$$ Dividing both sides by $\lambda = u'(c)$: $$\rho = f'(k) - (n + \delta + g_A) + \frac{u''(c)}{u'(c)}\frac{dc(t)}{dt}$$ Rearranging gives the general Euler equation: $$\frac{u''(c)}{u'(c)}\frac{dc(t)}{dt} = \rho - \left[f'(k) - (n + \delta + g_A)\right]$$
2
Substitute the explicit CRRA derivatives: We know for CRRA utility that: $$u'(c) = c^{-\varepsilon} \quad \text{and} \quad u''(c) = -\varepsilon c^{-\varepsilon-1}$$ Therefore the ratio $\dfrac{u''(c)}{u'(c)}$ simplifies neatly: $$\frac{u''(c)}{u'(c)} = \frac{-\varepsilon c^{-\varepsilon-1}}{c^{-\varepsilon}} = -\frac{\varepsilon}{c}$$
3
Substitute into the general equation with $f'(k) = \alpha k^{\alpha-1}$: $$-\frac{\varepsilon}{c}\frac{dc(t)}{dt} = \rho - \left[\alpha k^{\alpha-1} - (n + \delta + g_A)\right]$$ Multiply both sides by $-\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}$: $$\frac{1}{c}\frac{dc(t)}{dt} = \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\alpha k^{\alpha-1} - (n + \delta + g_A) - \rho\right]$$ Introducing the growth rate of consumption $g_c \equiv \dfrac{1}{c}\dfrac{dc(t)}{dt}$: $$\boxed{g_c = \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\alpha k^{\alpha-1} - \rho - n - \delta - g_A\right]}$$
Economic Meaning of the Ramsey Euler Equation:

• $\alpha k^{\alpha-1}$ is the marginal product of capital ($f'(k)$), representing the gross return to saving.
• $n + \delta + g_A$ are the break-even terms that capital must offset.
• $\rho$ is the rate of impatience (psychological cost of waiting).
• $\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon} = \text{IES}$ is the intertemporal elasticity of substitution, determining how strongly consumption growth responds to the net return to saving.

• If $\alpha k^{\alpha-1} - (n + \delta + g_A) > \rho$: The net return to saving exceeds impatience $\implies \mathbf{g_c > 0}$ (consumption grows over time).
• If $\alpha k^{\alpha-1} - (n + \delta + g_A) = \rho$: The return exactly equals impatience $\implies \mathbf{g_c = 0}$ (consumption is constant).
• If $\alpha k^{\alpha-1} - (n + \delta + g_A) < \rho$: The return is below impatience $\implies \mathbf{g_c < 0}$ (consumption declines over time).
Summary

Solving the Ramsey Model (3/3) — The Steady State & Complete System

At the Ramsey steady state, both per-effective-worker variables must be stationary. The steady state requires two simultaneous conditions:

$$\boxed{g_c = 0 \iff \frac{dc}{dt} = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{dk}{dt} = 0}$$

1. Determining Steady-State Capital $k^*$ (From the Euler Equation)

Setting $g_c = 0$ in the Euler equation gives:

$$\alpha (k^*)^{\alpha-1} = \rho + n + \delta + g_A \iff \boxed{f'(k^*) = \rho + n + \delta + g_A}$$

Solving explicitly for $k^*$ under Cobb-Douglas production:

$$\boxed{k^* = \left[\frac{\alpha}{\rho + n + \delta + g_A}\right]^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}}$$

2. Determining Steady-State Consumption $c^*$ (From Capital Accumulation)

Setting $\dfrac{dk}{dt} = 0$ in the capital accumulation equation ensures the capital stock does not change:

$$(k^*)^\alpha - c^* - (n + \delta + g_A)k^* = 0 \iff \boxed{c^* = (k^*)^\alpha - (n + \delta + g_A)k^*}$$

Note: $\dfrac{dc}{dt} = 0$ alone is not sufficient to define the steady state; $\dfrac{dk}{dt} = 0$ is equally required for the full macroeconomic equilibrium.

Ramsey Steady State vs. Solow Golden Rule:

• Solow Golden Rule: $f'(k^{**}) = n + \delta + g_A$
• Ramsey Steady State: $f'(k^*) = \rho + n + \delta + g_A$

Since households are impatient ($\rho > 0$), we have: $$f'(k^*) > f'(k^{**})$$ Because the production function exhibits strictly diminishing returns to capital ($f''(k) < 0$), a higher marginal product implies a strictly lower capital stock:

$$\boxed{k^* < k^{**}}$$

Dynamic Efficiency: In the Solow model, an economy could accumulate too much capital ($k^* > k^{**}$ if $s > \alpha$), wasting output in unproductive investment (dynamic inefficiency). In the Ramsey model, forward-looking households choose consumption optimally and will never over-accumulate capital beyond the Golden Rule: $k^* < k^{**}$ holds endogenously because $\rho > 0$.

Complete Dynamic System at the Optimum

1. Optimal Consumption Growth (Euler Equation):
$$g_c \equiv \frac{1}{c}\frac{dc}{dt} = \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\alpha k^{\alpha-1} - \rho - n - \delta - g_A\right]$$
2. Capital Accumulation (Resource Constraint):
$$\frac{dk}{dt} = k^\alpha - c - (n + \delta + g_A)k$$
3. Transversality Condition (Terminal Constraint):
$$\lim_{t \to \infty} e^{-\rho t}\,\lambda(t)\,k(t) = 0$$

Synthesis: Solow Model vs. Ramsey Model
