Chapter 1 — The Neoclassical Growth Model

The Solow Model

Capital accumulation, steady states, and long-run economic growth.

Core Foundations

Strong Assumptions

The Solow model uses three key assumptions to analyze long-term capital accumulation:

1
Constant Returns to Scale
Definition: Multiplying all inputs (capital $K$ and labour $L$) by any positive number $\lambda$ multiplies total production $Y$ by that same number:
$$F(\lambda K, \lambda L) = \lambda F(K, L) \qquad \forall \lambda > 0$$
Why it matters: By choosing $\lambda = 1/L$, we can write the entire production function in per-worker terms: $y = f(k)$.
2
Decreasing Marginal Returns of Inputs
★ Most Important
Definition: Adding an additional unit of capital (holding labour fixed) increases output, but by a smaller amount each time:
$$F_K > 0 \quad \text{(positive return)} \qquad \text{and} \qquad F_{KK} < 0 \quad \text{(diminishing return)}$$
Why it is the most important: This is why growth slows down. When capital is low, each new unit brings high gains. As capital grows, each extra unit adds less and less, eventually balancing out with depreciation and population growth.
Inada conditions: When $K \to 0$, marginal return is infinite ($\lim_{K\to 0} F_K = \infty$). When $K \to \infty$, it approaches zero ($\lim_{K\to\infty} F_K = 0$). This guarantees that a unique steady state $k^*$ exists.
3
Constant and Exogenous Saving Rate
Definition: Households save a fixed share $s \in (0,1)$ of total income $Y$:
$$S = sY \qquad \implies \qquad I = sY$$
Why it is assumed: In reality, people adjust how much they save according to interest rates and future needs. Taking $s$ as a constant parameter simplifies the model while keeping the main mechanisms clear.
Key Takeaways

Main Messages

Three essential conclusions from the Solow framework:

→
1. Each economy converges to a long-term steady-state path
Due to decreasing marginal returns, new investment eventually balances out the capital lost to depreciation and population dilution ($dk/dt = 0$). Regardless of its starting point, the economy naturally heads toward this stable steady state ($k^*$).
🌍
2. Steady states can be different from one country to another
Long-run capital and income depend on local parameters: saving rate ($s$), population growth ($n$), and depreciation ($\delta$). Countries with higher saving rates or lower population growth achieve a higher $k^*$, explaining persistent cross-country income differences.
⚡
3. The further away an economy is from its steady state, the higher its growth rate
When capital is very low compared to its steady state ($k \ll k^*$), the return on capital is high, making investment very productive and growth fast. As $k$ nears $k^*$, growth slows down. This property is called conditional convergence.
Equation

Production Function

Total output (GDP) is generated by physical capital and labour:

$$Y = F(K, L)$$
$Y$ — GDP (total production)
$K$ — stock of physical capital
$L$ — labour force
$F$ — production technology

Constant returns to scale

$$F(\lambda K, \lambda L) = \lambda F(K, L) \qquad \forall \lambda > 0$$

Positive and decreasing marginal returns

$$F_K > 0,\quad F_{KK} < 0,\quad F_L > 0,\quad F_{LL} < 0$$
$$\lim_{K\to 0}F_K = \infty, \quad \lim_{K\to\infty}F_K = 0, \quad \lim_{L\to 0}F_L = \infty, \quad \lim_{L\to\infty}F_L = 0$$
1
Apply constant returns to scale: set $\lambda = \dfrac{1}{L}$. $$F\!\left(\frac{K}{L},\; \frac{L}{L}\right) = \frac{1}{L}\,F(K,L) \implies F\!\left(\frac{K}{L},\,1\right) = \frac{Y}{L}$$
2
Define per-worker variables:

Capital per worker: $k = \dfrac{K}{L}$  |  Output per worker: $y = \dfrac{Y}{L}$

Define the function $f(k) \equiv F(k, 1)$. We directly obtain:

$$\boxed{y = f(k)}$$
3
Properties of $f(k)$: $f(0)=0$, $f'(k)>0$ (positive marginal product), and $f''(k) < 0$ (diminishing marginal returns).
Equation

Capital Accumulation

Total output is either consumed or invested. In a closed economy without a public sector, savings equal investment:

$$Y = C + I, \qquad I = S = sY$$
$s$ — saving rate
$\delta$ — capital depreciation rate
$g_L \equiv \frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt} = n$ — population growth rate
$k = K/L$ — capital per worker

A. Aggregate Capital $K$

  • Gross investment: $I = S = sY = sF(K,L)$ — increases the physical capital stock.
  • Depreciation: $\delta K$ — reduces the capital stock as machines wear out.

Net change in aggregate capital is gross investment minus depreciation:

$$\frac{dK}{dt} = I - \delta K = sF(K,L) - \delta K$$

B. Capital per Worker $k = K/L$

In per-worker terms, the fundamental law of motion is:

$$\frac{dk}{dt} = sf(k) - (\delta + n)k$$
1
Differentiate $k = \frac{K}{L}$ with respect to time $t$ using the quotient rule: $$\frac{dk}{dt} = \frac{\frac{dK}{dt} \cdot L - K \cdot \frac{dL}{dt}}{L^2} = \frac{1}{L}\frac{dK}{dt} - \frac{K}{L}\left(\frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt}\right)$$
2
Substitute the growth rates: We know $g_L \equiv \frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt} = n$ and $\frac{dK}{dt} = sF(K,L) - \delta K$: $$\frac{dk}{dt} = \frac{sF(K,L) - \delta K}{L} - n k$$
3
Simplify using $F(K,L)/L = f(k)$ and $K/L = k$: $$\frac{dk}{dt} = s f(k) - \delta k - n k$$
4
Collect terms: $$\boxed{\frac{dk}{dt} = sf(k) - (\delta + n)k}$$

C. Two Sides Interpretation

The change in capital per worker ($\frac{dk}{dt}$) equals gross investment per worker minus break-even investment.

  • $sf(k)$ = gross investment per worker $\to$ adds capital per worker.
  • $(\delta+n)k$ = break-even investment:
    • $\delta k$ = replacement of depreciated capital.
    • $nk$ = capital dilution (more workers means you need more capital just to keep $k$ constant).

This creates three cases that drive the Solow diagram:
• If $sf(k) > (\delta+n)k \implies \frac{dk}{dt} > 0 \implies k \uparrow$
• If $sf(k) = (\delta+n)k \implies \frac{dk}{dt} = 0 \implies k$ is constant (steady state)
• If $sf(k) < (\delta+n)k \implies \frac{dk}{dt} < 0 \implies k \downarrow$

Graph

The Solow Diagram

The Solow diagram. The concave curve $sf(k)$ meets the break-even line $(\delta+n)k$ at steady state $k^*$. Below $k^*$, investment exceeds break-even requirements, so capital grows. Above $k^*$, capital depreciates faster than new investment, so capital falls.

Question 1 — Capital and Labour Income Shares in GDP

Under perfect competition, profit-maximising firms hire each factor until its price equals its marginal product:

  • $r = \dfrac{\partial Y}{\partial K}$ : real interest rate / return to capital (marginal product of capital MPK)
  • $w = \dfrac{\partial Y}{\partial L}$ : real wage / return to labour (marginal product of labour MPL)

Total capital income paid across the entire economy is $r$ per unit $\times K$ units $= rK$. Differentiating $Y = K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}$ with respect to $K$:

$$r = \frac{\partial Y}{\partial K} = \alpha K^{\alpha-1}L^{1-\alpha}$$
$$rK = \alpha K^{\alpha-1}L^{1-\alpha} \cdot K = \alpha K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha} = \alpha Y$$

Divide by total GDP $Y$ to get capital's share in output:

$$\boxed{\frac{rK}{Y} = \alpha}$$

Similarly, the total wage bill paid to workers is $w$ per worker $\times L$ workers $= wL$. Differentiating $Y = K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}$ with respect to $L$:

$$w = \frac{\partial Y}{\partial L} = (1-\alpha)K^\alpha L^{-\alpha}$$
$$wL = (1-\alpha)K^\alpha L^{-\alpha} \cdot L = (1-\alpha)K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha} = (1-\alpha)Y$$

Divide by total GDP $Y$ to get labour's share in output:

$$\boxed{\frac{wL}{Y} = 1-\alpha}$$

Euler's Theorem (GDP Exhaustion):

$$rK + wL = \alpha Y + (1-\alpha)Y = Y$$

With constant returns to scale and competitive factor pricing, the entire GDP is completely exhausted by payments to capital and labour — there is no residual profit left.

Question 2 — Intensive Form: $f(k) = k^\alpha$

Output per worker is defined as $y = \dfrac{Y}{L}$. Substitute the production function:

$$y = \frac{K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}}{L} = K^\alpha L^{-\alpha} = \left(\frac{K}{L}\right)^\alpha$$

Since capital per worker is defined as $k = \dfrac{K}{L}$, we obtain the intensive production function:

$$\boxed{y = f(k) = k^\alpha}$$

Interpretation: Output per worker depends solely on capital per worker. Because $0 < \alpha < 1$, the function exhibits diminishing marginal returns to capital: $f'(k) = \alpha k^{\alpha-1} > 0$ and $f''(k) = \alpha(\alpha-1)k^{\alpha-2} < 0$.

Question 3 — Steady-State Capital per Worker: $k^*$

The capital accumulation equation in intensive form is $\dfrac{dk}{dt} = s f(k) - (\delta + n)k = s k^\alpha - (\delta + n)k$.

At the steady state, capital per worker is constant, so $\boxed{\dfrac{dk}{dt} = 0}$:

$$s (k^*)^\alpha - (\delta + n)k^* = 0 \implies s (k^*)^\alpha = (\delta + n)k^*$$

Divide both sides by $(k^*)^\alpha$:

$$(k^*)^{1-\alpha} = \frac{s}{\delta + n}$$

Raise both sides to the power $\dfrac{1}{1-\alpha}$:

$$\boxed{k^* = \left(\frac{s}{\delta + n}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}}$$
🎓 Lecture Focus — What Happens at the Steady State ($k^*$)?

In growth theory, the steady state is a dynamic long-run equilibrium. Capital per worker stops changing ($\frac{dk}{dt} = 0$), but economic activity continues at full pace:

• Gross investment is strictly positive ($I > 0$): Households continuously save a share $s$ of their income, so new capital is actively manufactured ($sf(k^*) > 0$). It is an exam trap to believe that $\frac{dk}{dt} = 0$ means investment has stopped!

• Net capital accumulation per worker is zero ($\frac{dk}{dt} = 0$): Gross investment exactly matches break-even investment $(\delta + n)k^*$, which fulfills two distinct needs:
  1. Capital replacement ($\delta k^*$): replacing worn-out machines that depreciated.
  2. Capital dilution ($n k^*$): equipping each new incoming worker with $k^*$ units of capital so that the capital-labour ratio does not decline.

• Intensive variables plateau ($g_k = 0, g_y = 0$): Capital per worker $k^*$ and GDP per capita $y^* = (k^*)^\alpha$ remain flat over time: $$\boxed{g_k \equiv \frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt} = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad g_y \equiv \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} = 0}$$ Without technological progress, living standards cannot experience sustained long-run growth.

• Aggregate variables expand at rate $n$ ($g_K = n, g_Y = n$): Total capital $K = k^* L$ and total output $Y = y^* L$ expand at the population growth rate: $$\boxed{g_K \equiv \frac{1}{K}\frac{dK}{dt} = n \qquad \text{and} \qquad g_Y \equiv \frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt} = n}$$ The overall macroeconomy continues to grow, but purely due to demographic expansion, not rising individual wealth.

• Global stability: If an economy begins with $k < k^*$, investment per worker exceeds break-even requirements ($sf(k) > (\delta+n)k$), so capital accumulates ($\frac{dk}{dt} > 0$). If $k > k^*$, depreciation and dilution dominate, so capital decumulates ($\frac{dk}{dt} < 0$). The economy always converges to $k^*$.

Question 4 — Growth Rate of GDP per Capita at $k^*$

GDP per capita is $y = f(k) = k^\alpha$.

Step 1 — Full time derivative: Differentiate with respect to time using the chain rule:

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = \alpha k^{\alpha-1}\frac{dk}{dt}$$

Divide by $y = k^\alpha$ to express the proportional growth rate $g_y \equiv \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt}$:

$$g_y \equiv \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{\alpha k^{\alpha-1}\frac{dk}{dt}}{k^\alpha} = \alpha \left(\frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt}\right) = \alpha g_k$$

Step 2 — At the steady state ($k = k^*$):

By definition of the steady state, capital per worker is constant, so $\frac{dk}{dt} = 0$. Writing the full derivative with the explicit zero term:

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = \alpha (k^*)^{\alpha-1} \underbrace{\frac{dk}{dt}}_{=0} = 0$$

Therefore, the growth rate of GDP per capita at the steady state is zero:

$$\boxed{g_y \equiv \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} = 0}$$

Interpretation: In the baseline Solow model without technological progress, living standards stagnate in the long run. Capital accumulation alone cannot sustain permanent growth in per-capita income due to diminishing returns.

Question 5 — Growth Rate of Total GDP at $k^*$

Total GDP is output per worker multiplied by total labour: $Y = y \cdot L$.

Step 1 — Full time derivative: Differentiate with respect to time using the product rule:

$$\frac{dY}{dt} = \frac{dy}{dt}L + y\frac{dL}{dt}$$

Divide both sides by $Y = yL$ to obtain the growth rate of total GDP:

$$g_Y \equiv \frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt} = \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} + \frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt} = g_y + g_L$$

Step 2 — At the steady state ($k = k^*$):

Substitute the steady-state growth rate of GDP per capita ($g_y = 0$) and the population growth rate ($g_L = n$):

$$g_Y \equiv \frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt} = \underbrace{g_y}_{=0} + \underbrace{g_L}_{=n} = n$$
$$\boxed{g_Y \equiv \frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt} = n}$$

Similarly for total capital $K = k \cdot L \implies g_K = g_k + g_L = 0 + n = n$.

Interpretation: Total GDP grows at the rate of population growth $n$. The economy expands in scale because the workforce grows, not because workers become richer.

Question 6 — Expressing $s$ in Terms of $k^*$, $n$, and $\delta$

At the steady state, $\frac{dk}{dt} = 0 \implies s (k^*)^\alpha = (\delta + n)k^*$.

Divide both sides by $(k^*)^\alpha$:

$$\boxed{s = (\delta + n)(k^*)^{1-\alpha}}$$

This formula gives the saving rate required to maintain any target capital stock $k^*$ against depreciation and population dilution.

Question 7 — Growth Rate of $k$ in Terms of $k/k^*$

Start from the capital accumulation equation: $\dfrac{dk}{dt} = s k^\alpha - (\delta + n)k$.

Divide by $k$ to express the proportional growth rate of capital per worker $g_k \equiv \frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt}$:

$$g_k \equiv \frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt} = s k^{\alpha-1} - (\delta + n)$$

Substitute $s = (\delta + n)(k^*)^{1-\alpha}$ from Question 6:

$$g_k = (\delta + n)(k^*)^{1-\alpha} k^{\alpha-1} - (\delta + n) = (\delta + n)\left[(k^*)^{1-\alpha}k^{\alpha-1} - 1\right]$$

Since $(k^*)^{1-\alpha}k^{\alpha-1} = \left(\dfrac{k^*}{k}\right)^{1-\alpha}$, we obtain:

$$\boxed{g_k \equiv \frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt} = (\delta + n)\left[\left(\frac{k^*}{k}\right)^{\!1-\alpha} - 1\right]}$$

Equivalently, written in terms of $k/k^*$:

$$\boxed{g_k \equiv \frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt} = (\delta + n)\left[\left(\frac{k}{k^*}\right)^{\!\alpha-1} - 1\right]}$$

Interpretation & Convergence Dynamics:

  • If $k < k^*$: $\dfrac{k^*}{k} > 1 \implies \boxed{g_k > 0}$ (capital per worker accumulates).
  • If $k = k^*$: $\dfrac{k^*}{k} = 1 \implies \boxed{g_k = 0}$ (steady state is maintained).
  • If $k > k^*$: $\dfrac{k^*}{k} < 1 \implies \boxed{g_k < 0}$ (capital per worker decumulates).

This proves that the steady state $k^*$ is globally stable.

Question 8 — Growth Rate of GDP per Capita Outside the Steady State

From Question 4, we established that $g_y \equiv \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} = \alpha g_k$.

Substitute $g_k$ from Question 7:

$$\boxed{g_y \equiv \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} = \alpha(\delta + n)\left[\left(\frac{k^*}{k}\right)^{\!1-\alpha} - 1\right]}$$

Interpretation:

  • If $k < k^*$: GDP per capita grows ($g_y > 0$).
  • If $k = k^*$: GDP per capita is stationary ($g_y = 0$).
  • If $k > k^*$: GDP per capita shrinks ($g_y < 0$).

As the economy nears $k^*$, transitional growth slows down continuously and converges toward zero.

Question 9 — Growth Rate of Total GDP Outside the Steady State

From Question 5, total GDP growth is the sum of per-capita growth and population growth:

$$g_Y \equiv \frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt} = g_y + g_L = g_y + n$$

Substitute $g_y$ from Question 8:

$$\boxed{g_Y \equiv \frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt} = \alpha(\delta + n)\left[\left(\frac{k^*}{k}\right)^{\!1-\alpha} - 1\right] + n}$$

Interpretation:

When an economy is accumulating capital ($k < k^*$), total GDP grows faster than population growth ($g_Y > n$). As capital per worker approaches its steady state ($k \to k^*$), transitional growth dries up ($g_y \to 0$), and total GDP growth converges smoothly to population growth:

$$\boxed{g_Y \longrightarrow n}$$

Concept 3 / 3

Adding Total Factor Productivity (TFP)

To generate permanent long-run growth in per-capita living standards, we introduce labour-augmenting (Harrod-neutral) technological progress:

$$Y = (AL)^{1-\alpha} K^\alpha \qquad \text{with} \qquad 0 < \alpha < 1$$
$L$ — number of actual workers
$A$ — productivity of each worker (technology)
$AL$ — effective labour supply (labour measured in efficiency units)
$g_A \equiv \frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt}$ — rate of technological progress
$g_L \equiv \frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt} = n$ — population growth rate
$k = \frac{K}{AL}$ — capital per effective worker
$y = \frac{Y}{AL} = k^\alpha$ — output per effective worker
$Y/L = A \cdot y = A k^\alpha$ — output per actual worker (real living standards)

Growth of Effective Labour Supply ($AL$)

Differentiating $AL$ with respect to time using the product rule:

$$\frac{d(AL)}{dt} = L\frac{dA}{dt} + A\frac{dL}{dt}$$

Divide by $AL$ to obtain the growth rate of effective labour $g_{AL} \equiv \frac{1}{AL}\frac{d(AL)}{dt}$ :

$$g_{AL} \equiv \frac{1}{AL}\frac{d(AL)}{dt} = \frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt} + \frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt} = g_A + g_L = g_A + n$$

Capital Accumulation per Effective Worker

$$\frac{dk}{dt} = sf(k) - (\delta + n + g_A)k$$

(Note: the parameter $g_A$ is also denoted $g$ in lecture slides, representing the constant technological progress rate).

1
Differentiate $k = \frac{K}{AL}$ with respect to time using the quotient rule: $$\frac{dk}{dt} = \frac{\frac{dK}{dt}(AL) - K \frac{d(AL)}{dt}}{(AL)^2} = \frac{1}{AL}\frac{dK}{dt} - k\,\frac{\frac{d(AL)}{dt}}{AL}$$
2
Growth of effective labour: Substitute $g_{AL} \equiv \frac{1}{AL}\frac{d(AL)}{dt} = g_A + g_L = g_A + n$.
3
Substitute aggregate accumulation $\frac{dK}{dt} = sY - \delta K$: $$\frac{dk}{dt} = \frac{sY - \delta K}{AL} - (n+g_A)k = s\,\frac{Y}{AL} - \delta k - (n+g_A)k$$

Using $y = \frac{Y}{AL} = f(k) = k^\alpha$:

$$\boxed{\frac{dk}{dt} = sf(k) - (\delta + n + g_A)k}$$

Two sides interpretation: $sf(k)$ is gross investment per effective worker. The term $(\delta+n+g_A)k$ is break-even investment, combining:
• $\delta k$ : physical depreciation (worn-out machines).
• $n k$ : demographic dilution (capital needed to equip new workers).
• $g_A k$ : technological dilution (as each worker becomes more productive, more capital is needed just to keep the capital-efficiency ratio $k = \frac{K}{AL}$ constant).

Question 1 — Intensive Form: $f(k) = k^\alpha$ with $k = \dfrac{K}{AL}$

The aggregate production function with labour-augmenting technological progress is:

$$Y = (AL)^{1-\alpha} K^\alpha$$

Output per effective worker is denoted as $y \equiv \dfrac{Y}{AL}$. Divide both sides by $AL$:

$$y = \frac{Y}{AL} = \frac{(AL)^{1-\alpha} K^\alpha}{AL} = \frac{K^\alpha}{(AL)^\alpha} = \left(\frac{K}{AL}\right)^\alpha$$

Since capital per effective worker is defined as $k = \dfrac{K}{AL}$, we obtain the intensive form:

$$\boxed{y = f(k) = k^\alpha}$$
Crucial Distinction — Two Different Output Concepts:
  • $y = \dfrac{Y}{AL}$ : output per effective worker (adjusted for worker efficiency).
  • $Y/L = A \cdot y = A k^\alpha$ : output per actual worker (real GDP per capita / living standards).
Question 2 — Steady-State Capital per Effective Worker: $k^*$

The capital accumulation equation in intensive form is $\dfrac{dk}{dt} = s k^\alpha - (\delta + n + g_A)k$.

At the steady state, capital per effective worker is constant, so $\boxed{\dfrac{dk}{dt} = 0}$:

$$s (k^*)^\alpha - (\delta + n + g_A)k^* = 0 \implies s (k^*)^\alpha = (\delta + n + g_A)k^*$$

Divide both sides by $(k^*)^\alpha$:

$$(k^*)^{1-\alpha} = \frac{s}{\delta + n + g_A}$$

Raise both sides to the power $\dfrac{1}{1-\alpha}$:

$$\boxed{k^* = \left(\frac{s}{\delta + n + g_A}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}}$$

Interpretation: $k^*$ is the long-run equilibrium level of capital per effective worker. Investment per effective worker $s f(k^*)$ balances physical depreciation ($\delta k^*$), population growth ($n k^*$), and technological dilution ($g_A k^*$).

Question 3 — Growth Rates of Output per Effective Worker ($g_y$) and GDP per Capita ($g_{Y/L}$) at $k^*$

At the steady state, capital per effective worker is stationary: $k = k^* \implies g_k \equiv \frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt} = 0$. We explicitly distinguish both output measures:

Part A — Output per Effective Worker: $y = \frac{Y}{AL} = k^\alpha$

Differentiate $y = k^\alpha$ with respect to time:

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = \alpha k^{\alpha-1} \frac{dk}{dt}$$

Divide by $y = k^\alpha$ to obtain its proportional growth rate:

$$g_y \equiv \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} = \alpha \left(\frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt}\right) = \alpha g_k$$

At the steady state, $\frac{dk}{dt} = 0$ (so $g_k = 0$):

$$\boxed{g_y \equiv \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} = \alpha \underbrace{g_k}_{=0} = 0}$$

Conclusion: Output per effective worker is stationary at the steady state.

Part B — Output per Actual Worker / GDP per Capita: $Y/L = A \cdot y = A k^\alpha$

Differentiate $Y/L = A k^\alpha$ with respect to time using the product rule (writing the full derivative before setting anything to zero):

$$\frac{d(Y/L)}{dt} = \frac{dA}{dt}k^\alpha + A \alpha k^{\alpha-1}\frac{dk}{dt}$$

Divide by $Y/L = A k^\alpha$ to obtain the general growth rate of GDP per capita:

$$g_{Y/L} \equiv \frac{1}{Y/L}\frac{d(Y/L)}{dt} = \frac{\frac{dA}{dt}k^\alpha}{A k^\alpha} + \frac{A\alpha k^{\alpha-1}\frac{dk}{dt}}{A k^\alpha} = \frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt} + \alpha \frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt} = g_A + \alpha g_k$$

At the steady state, substitute $\frac{dk}{dt} = 0$ (so $g_k = 0$) and $\frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt} = g_A$:

$$\frac{d(Y/L)}{dt} = \frac{dA}{dt}(k^*)^\alpha + A\alpha(k^*)^{\alpha-1}\underbrace{\frac{dk}{dt}}_{=0} = \frac{dA}{dt}(k^*)^\alpha$$
$$\boxed{g_{Y/L} \equiv \frac{1}{Y/L}\frac{d(Y/L)}{dt} = g_A + \alpha \underbrace{g_k}_{=0} = g_A}$$

Interpretation: While output per effective worker $y^*$ is stationary, output per actual worker (living standards) grows continuously at the rate of technological progress $g_A$ because workers become continuously more productive over time.

Question 4 — All Growth Rates at the Steady State $k^*$

At the steady state $k = k^*$, we have $\dfrac{dk}{dt} = 0$ (so $g_k = 0$). We derive the three key growth rates the professor presented.

Part A — Growth Rate of Output per Effective Worker: $y = Y/(AL) = k^\alpha$

Differentiate $y = k^\alpha$ with respect to time:

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = \alpha k^{\alpha-1}\frac{dk}{dt}$$

Divide by $y = k^\alpha$ to obtain the proportional growth rate:

$$g_y \equiv \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} = \alpha \left(\frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt}\right) = \alpha g_k$$

At the steady state, $\dfrac{dk}{dt} = 0$ (so $g_k = 0$):

$$\boxed{g_y \equiv \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} = \alpha \underbrace{g_k}_{=0} = 0}$$

Conclusion: Output per effective worker is stationary at the steady state — it neither grows nor falls.

Part B — Growth Rate of GDP per Capita (Living Standards): $Y/L = A \cdot y = A k^\alpha$

Differentiate $Y/L = A k^\alpha$ with respect to time using the product rule. Write the full derivative first:

$$\frac{d(Y/L)}{dt} = \frac{dA}{dt}k^\alpha + A\alpha k^{\alpha-1}\frac{dk}{dt}$$

Divide by $Y/L = Ak^\alpha$ to obtain the general growth rate of GDP per capita:

$$g_{Y/L} \equiv \frac{1}{Y/L}\frac{d(Y/L)}{dt} = \frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt} + \alpha\frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt} = g_A + \alpha g_k$$

At the steady state, substitute $\dfrac{dk}{dt} = 0$ explicitly:

$$\frac{d(Y/L)}{dt} = \frac{dA}{dt}(k^*)^\alpha + A\alpha(k^*)^{\alpha-1}\underbrace{\frac{dk}{dt}}_{=0} = \frac{dA}{dt}(k^*)^\alpha$$
$$\boxed{g_{Y/L} \equiv \frac{1}{Y/L}\frac{d(Y/L)}{dt} = g_A + \alpha\underbrace{g_k}_{=0} = g_A}$$

Conclusion: Even though output per effective worker $y$ is stationary, GDP per capita (living standards) grows at rate $g_A$ because workers continuously become more productive as technology improves.

Part C — Growth Rate of Total GDP: $Y = (Y/L) \cdot L$

Total GDP is output per worker times the labour force. Differentiate using the product rule:

$$\frac{dY}{dt} = \frac{d(Y/L)}{dt} \cdot L + (Y/L)\cdot\frac{dL}{dt}$$

Divide by $Y = (Y/L)L$ to obtain the general growth rate of total GDP:

$$g_Y \equiv \frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt} = g_{Y/L} + g_L$$

At the steady state, substitute $g_{Y/L} = g_A$ (from Part B) and $g_L = n$:

$$\boxed{g_Y \equiv \frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt} = g_A + n}$$

Conclusion: Total GDP grows at the combined rate of technological progress and population growth. Output expands because each worker produces more ($g_A$) and because the labour force grows ($n$).

Summary of Growth Rates at the Balanced Growth Path:
  • $g_y = 0$  — output per effective worker is stationary.
  • $g_{Y/L} = g_A$  — living standards (GDP per capita) grow at the rate of technological progress.
  • $g_Y = g_A + n$  — total GDP grows at the rate of technology plus population growth.
Question 5 — Expressing $s$ in Terms of $k^*$, $n$, $g_A$, and $\delta$

$$\boxed{s = (\delta + n + g_A)(k^*)^{1-\alpha}}$$

This links the saving rate directly to the steady-state capital stock per effective worker and the effective depreciation rate $(\delta + n + g_A)$.

Question 6 — Growth Rate of $k$ in Terms of $k/k^*$

Start from the capital accumulation equation: $\dfrac{dk}{dt} = s k^\alpha - (\delta + n + g_A)k$.

Divide by $k$ to obtain the proportional growth rate of capital per effective worker $g_k \equiv \frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt}$:

$$g_k \equiv \frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt} = s k^{\alpha-1} - (\delta + n + g_A)$$

Substitute $s = (\delta + n + g_A)(k^*)^{1-\alpha}$ from Question 5:

$$g_k = (\delta + n + g_A)(k^*)^{1-\alpha}k^{\alpha-1} - (\delta + n + g_A) = (\delta + n + g_A)\left[(k^*)^{1-\alpha}k^{\alpha-1} - 1\right]$$

$$\boxed{g_k \equiv \frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt} = (\delta + n + g_A)\left[\left(\frac{k^*}{k}\right)^{\!1-\alpha} - 1\right]}$$

Convergence Dynamics: When $k < k^*$, $g_k > 0$; when $k = k^*$, $g_k = 0$; when $k > k^*$, $g_k < 0$. Capital per effective worker always converges to $k^*$.

Question 7 — Growth Rate of GDP per Capita Outside the Steady State

Connection to Income Shares: Multiplying factor prices by factor quantities confirms Euler's exhaustion: $$rK = \alpha k^{\alpha-1}(kAL) = \alpha k^\alpha (AL) = \alpha Y \qquad \text{and} \qquad wL = (1-\alpha)A k^\alpha L = (1-\alpha)Y$$

Is a Higher Saving Rate Always Better?

The diagram below shows the Solow model with technological progress for three different saving rates: $s_{\text{low}}$, $s$, and $s_{\text{high}}$. Each rate implies a different steady-state capital per effective worker.

Steady-state capital depends on $s$: higher $s$ shifts the investment curve up, raising $k^*$. But consumption per effective worker $c^* = f(k^*) - (\delta+n+g_A)k^*$ is not maximised by the highest $k^*$.
Observation 1 — A higher $s$ raises $k^*$:
Moving from $s_{\text{low}}$ to $s_{\text{high}}$ shifts the investment curve upward, pushing the intersection point with the break-even line to the right. Capital per effective worker, and therefore output per effective worker, are permanently higher.
Observation 2 — But a higher $s$ compresses consumption:
Steady-state consumption per effective worker is $c^* = f(k^*) - (\delta+n+g_A)k^*$. Raising $s$ above some threshold means too much output is channelled into investment: households sacrifice consumption today and in the long run. Somewhere between $s_{\text{low}}$ and $s_{\text{high}}$ lies the optimal $s$.
Economic Question — What is the optimal saving rate?
Which saving rate $s$ maximises $c^* = f(k^*) - (\delta+n+g_A)k^*$? Graphically, it is the saving rate for which the vertical gap between $f(k)$ and the break-even line $(\delta+n+g_A)k$ is greatest — i.e., where their slopes are equal: $$f'(k^{**}) = \delta + n + g_A$$ This condition defines the Golden Rule capital stock $k^{**}$ and, for Cobb-Douglas, implies the Golden Rule saving rate $s = \alpha$.
Golden Rule 1/2

The Saving Rate that Maximises Consumption

Question: Is there a saving rate $s$ that maximises steady-state consumption per effective worker?

Step 1 — Express $s$ in terms of $k^*$

$$s = (\delta+n+g_A)\,k^{*\,1-\alpha}$$

Step 2 — Write steady-state consumption per effective worker

$$c^* = (1-s)f(k^*) = f(k^*) - (\delta+n+g_A)k^* = (k^*)^\alpha - (\delta+n+g_A)k^*$$

Step 3 — Maximise $c^*$ with respect to $k^*$

Differentiate with respect to $k^*$ and set to 0:

$$\frac{dc^*}{dk^*} = f'(k^{**}) - (\delta+n+g_A) = 0 \implies f'(k^{**}) = \delta+n+g_A$$

For Cobb-Douglas, $f'(k) = \alpha k^{\alpha-1}$:

$$\alpha (k^{**})^{\alpha-1} = \delta+n+g_A \implies (k^{**})^{1-\alpha} = \frac{\alpha}{\delta+n+g_A}$$
$$k^{**} = \left(\frac{\alpha}{\delta+n+g_A}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{1-\alpha}} \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad \boxed{s = \alpha}$$
The Golden Rule: When the saving rate equals capital's share of income ($s = \alpha$), steady-state consumption per effective worker reaches its maximum ($k^* = k^{**}$).
✎ Golden Rule 2/2
A Higher Saving Rate Is Not Always Better

Consider an economy where the golden rule is not respected:

  • 1.
    If $s > \alpha$ (over-saving): What would happen to per capita consumption across all generations if the government enforced the golden rule saving rate $s = \alpha$?
  • 2.
    If $s < \alpha$ (under-saving): What happens if the government raises the saving rate to $s = \alpha$?
Case 1 — $s > \alpha$ (Dynamic Inefficiency)

When $s > \alpha$, the economy is saving too much. Its capital stock is higher than the golden rule level ($k^* > k^{**}$), meaning too much output is spent just maintaining the capital stock rather than being consumed.

If the saving rate is lowered to $s = \alpha$:

• Immediately: Households save less, so they consume more output right away.

• In the long run: Capital settles at $k^{**}$, where steady-state consumption is at its highest.

Result: Every generation (present and future) consumes more. This is a Pareto improvement.

Case 2 — $s < \alpha$ (Dynamic Efficiency)

When $s < \alpha$, the economy has less capital than the golden rule level ($k^* < k^{**}$).

If the saving rate is increased to $s = \alpha$:

• In the short run: Households must save more today, which lowers their current consumption.

• In the long run: More capital accumulates, eventually leading to higher steady-state consumption for future generations.

Result: Current generations make a sacrifice for the benefit of future generations. This is not a Pareto improvement; it represents an intergenerational trade-off.
