Chapter 1a
Chapter 1 — Neoclassical Growth Model

The Solow Model

A foundational framework for understanding long-run capital accumulation and steady-state growth.

§1

Production Function

The economy produces a single good using two inputs:

$$Y = F(K,\, L)$$
$Y$ — total output / GDP
$K$ — stock of physical capital
$L$ — labour
$F$ — production technology
§2

Three Core Assumptions

A1
Constant Returns to Scale (CRS). Doubling all inputs exactly doubles output. $$F(\lambda K,\, \lambda L) = \lambda\, F(K,L) \qquad \forall\,\lambda > 0$$
A2
Positive and diminishing marginal products. $F_K > 0$, $F_L > 0$, $F_{KK} < 0$, $F_{LL} < 0$ — each additional unit of input adds less and less.
A3
Inada conditions. $\lim_{K\to 0} F_K = +\infty$, $\lim_{K\to\infty} F_K = 0$ — the marginal product of capital starts infinite and vanishes. Same for labour.
§3

Production Per Worker

Divide everything by $L$ to work in per-worker terms:

$$k = \frac{K}{L}, \qquad y = \frac{Y}{L}, \qquad y = f(k)$$

By CRS, the intensive form $f(k)$ is concave: $f(0)=0$, $f'(k)>0$, $f''(k)<0$.

Start from CRS: $F(\lambda K,\lambda L)=\lambda F(K,L)$. Set $\lambda = 1/L$:

$$F\!\left(\frac{K}{L},1\right) = \frac{1}{L}\,F(K,L) = \frac{Y}{L} = y$$

Define $f(k) \equiv F(k,1)$. Then $\boxed{y = f(k)}$.

§4

Goods Market Clearing

Output is either consumed or invested. With a constant saving rate $s \in (0,1)$:

$sY$ — savings = investment
$(1{-}s)Y$ — consumption
$$Y = C + I, \qquad I = sY$$

In per-worker terms: $i = sy = sf(k)$.

Households save fraction $s$ of income: $S = sY$. In a closed economy, $S = I$, so $I = sY$.

Consumption: $C = Y - I = (1-s)Y$. Dividing by $L$: $i = I/L = sy = sf(k)$.

§5

Labour Market

Labour grows at an exogenous rate $n \geq 0$:

$$\dot{L} = nL \implies L(t) = L(0)\,e^{nt}$$
Exogenous: Population growth is taken as given — the model does not explain it.
§6

Capital Accumulation

Capital evolves as gross investment minus depreciation:

$$\dot{K} = I - \delta K = sY - \delta K$$
$\delta$ — depreciation rate $(0 < \delta < 1)$
$\delta K$ — capital that wears out each period
§7

Law of Motion of Capital Per Worker

The central equation of the model:

$$\dot{k} = sf(k) - (\delta + n)k$$
Interpretation: $k$ rises when investment $sf(k)$ exceeds break-even investment $(\delta+n)k$, i.e.\ what is needed to replace depreciated capital and equip new workers.

Recall $k = K/L$. Differentiate with respect to time:

$$\dot{k} = \frac{\dot{K}}{L} - \frac{K\dot{L}}{L^2} = \frac{\dot{K}}{L} - k\frac{\dot{L}}{L}$$

Substitute $\dot{K} = sY - \delta K$ and $\dot{L}/L = n$:

$$\dot{k} = \frac{sY - \delta K}{L} - nk = sy - \delta k - nk$$

Since $y = f(k)$:

$$\boxed{\dot{k} = sf(k) - (\delta + n)k}$$

The term $(\delta + n)k$ is break-even investment — what is needed just to keep $k$ constant.

§8

Steady State

The steady state $k^*$ is the long-run equilibrium where $\dot{k} = 0$:

$$sf(k^*) = (\delta + n)k^*$$

At $k^*$, investment exactly covers break-even needs. Capital per worker, output per worker, and consumption per worker are all constant.

Define $g(k) = sf(k) - (\delta+n)k$.

At $k=0$: $g(0)=0$. Inada gives $f'(0)=+\infty$, so $g'(0) = +\infty$. The curve starts steep.

For large $k$: Inada gives $f'(k)\to 0$, so $g'(k) = sf'(k)-(\delta+n) < 0$ eventually.

By continuity and strict concavity of $f$, there is a unique interior crossing $k^*>0$ where $g(k^*)=0$.

§9

The Solow Diagram

The diagram plots $sf(k)$ and $(\delta+n)k$ against $k$. Their intersection defines $k^*$.

The Solow diagram. $sf(k)$ (concave, green) meets the break-even line $(\delta+n)k$ at the unique steady state $k^*$. Arrows indicate convergence from any initial $k > 0$.
§10

Convergence Dynamics

k < k*
$sf(k) > (\delta+n)k \implies \dot{k} > 0$ — capital per worker rises toward $k^*$.
k > k*
$sf(k) < (\delta+n)k \implies \dot{k} < 0$ — capital per worker falls toward $k^*$.
The Solow model exhibits global stability: regardless of the starting point $k(0)>0$, the economy converges to $k^*$.
§11

Steady-State Output and Consumption

Once $k^*$ is determined, all quantities follow:

$y^* = f(k^*)$ — output per worker
$c^* = (1{-}s)f(k^*)$ — consumption per worker
$i^* = sf(k^*)$ — investment per worker
$\dot{k}=0$ — no net change in $k$
In steady state, aggregate $Y$, $K$, $C$ grow at rate $n$ — only per-worker quantities are constant.
§12

Comparative Statics — Saving Rate

A rise in $s$ shifts $sf(k)$ upward:

$$s' > s \implies k^{**} > k^*$$
Higher saving → higher $k^*$ → higher $y^*$. But the long-run growth rate remains zero — only the income level changes. Saving cannot sustain permanent growth.
§13

Comparative Statics — Population Growth

Higher $n$ steepens the break-even line:

$$n' > n \implies k^{**} < k^*$$
Higher population growth → lower $k^*$ → lower $y^*$. Faster-growing populations have less capital per worker in steady state. Consistent with cross-country evidence.
§14

The Golden Rule

Which saving rate $s$ maximises steady-state consumption per worker?

Steady-state consumption: $c^* = f(k^*) - (\delta+n)k^*$. Maximise over $k^*$:

$$f'(k^*_{\text{GR}}) = \delta + n$$
Golden Rule: Optimal steady state has $MPK = \delta + n$. The corresponding saving rate $s_{GR}$ satisfies $s_{GR}\,f(k^*_{GR}) = (\delta+n)k^*_{GR}$.
§15

Essential Chain to Remember

The complete logical flow of the Solow model.

Primitives
$F(K,L)$ with CRS  ·  saving rate $s$  ·  depreciation $\delta$  ·  population growth $n$
↓
Intensive form
$y = f(k)$, concave  ·  $i = sf(k)$
↓
Law of Motion
$\dot{k} = sf(k) - (\delta + n)k$
↓
Steady State — $\dot{k}=0$
$sf(k^*) = (\delta+n)k^*$
↓
Long-Run Values
$y^* = f(k^*)$  ·  $c^* = (1-s)f(k^*)$  ·  $g_y = 0$
↓
Comparative Statics
$\uparrow s \Rightarrow \uparrow k^*$  ·  $\uparrow n \Rightarrow \downarrow k^*$  ·  Golden Rule: $f'(k_{GR}^*) = \delta+n$
