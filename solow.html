Chapter 1 — The Neoclassical Growth Model

The Solow Model

Capital accumulation, steady states, and long-run growth dynamics.

Foundation

Strong Assumptions

The Solow model is built on three strong simplifying assumptions. They make the model tractable and generate clean predictions — but at the cost of realism.

1
Constant Returns to Scale
If you double all inputs (capital and labour), you exactly double output. Formally: $F(\lambda K, \lambda L) = \lambda F(K,L)$ for any $\lambda > 0$.
Why it matters: CRS allows us to reduce the two-variable problem to a single variable by working in per-worker terms — dividing everything by $L$ to get $y = f(k)$.
2
Decreasing Marginal Returns of Inputs
★ Most important
Each additional unit of capital (holding labour fixed) adds less and less to output: $F_K > 0$ but $F_{KK} < 0$. Same for labour.
Why it’s the most important: Diminishing returns is the engine of convergence. When capital is scarce, its return is high → investment is profitable → capital accumulates fast. As capital grows, its return falls → growth slows down → the economy converges to a steady state. Without this assumption, there is no steady state.
The Inada conditions formalise the extremes: $\lim_{K\to 0}F_K = \infty$ and $\lim_{K\to\infty}F_K = 0$, ensuring $k^*$ always exists and is unique.
3
Constant and Exogenous Saving Rate
Households save a fixed fraction $s \in (0,1)$ of their income, regardless of the interest rate or their wealth. $s$ is set externally — the model does not explain why people save.
Why it’s strong: In reality, households optimise intertemporally (Ramsey model). Here, $s = \text{const}$ is a major simplification that rules out optimising behaviour, but it keeps the model analytically clean.
Big Picture

Main Messages

Three core predictions that follow directly from the model’s structure.

→
Each economy converges to a long-term steady state
Because of diminishing returns (A2), the marginal product of capital falls as $k$ rises. At some point, new investment only compensates for depreciation and population dilution — net growth of $k$ hits zero. This long-run equilibrium $k^*$ is globally stable: the economy always ends up there regardless of where it starts.
🌎
Steady states differ across countries
Countries differ in their saving rate $s$, population growth $n$, and depreciation $\delta$. Since $k^* = \left(\tfrac{s}{\delta+n}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)}$ (Cobb-Douglas case), a country that saves more or grows more slowly will have a higher $k^*$ — and therefore higher income per capita in the long run. The model thus rationalises persistent income differences across countries.
⚡
The further from the steady state, the faster the growth
When $k \ll k^*$, capital is scarce, its marginal product is very high, and investment is very profitable → $k$ grows rapidly. As $k \to k^*$, the marginal product falls and growth slows. This predicts conditional convergence: poor countries should grow faster than rich ones — conditional on having the same steady state. This is broadly supported by data.
Equation

Production Function

$$Y = F(K, L)$$
$Y$ — GDP
$K$ — stock of capital
$L$ — labour

Constant returns to scale

$$F(\lambda K, \lambda L) = \lambda F(K, L) \qquad \text{for all } \lambda > 0$$

Positive and decreasing marginal returns

$$F_K > 0,\quad F_{KK} < 0,\quad F_L > 0,\quad F_{LL} < 0$$
$$\lim_{K\to 0}F_K = \infty, \quad \lim_{K\to\infty}F_K = 0, \quad \lim_{L\to 0}F_L = \infty, \quad \lim_{L\to\infty}F_L = 0$$
1
Apply CRS with $\lambda = 1/L$. $$F\!\left(\frac{K}{L},\; \frac{L}{L}\right) = \frac{1}{L}\,F(K,L) \implies F\!\left(\frac{K}{L},\,1\right) = \frac{Y}{L}$$
2
Define the intensive-form function $f(k) \equiv F(k,\,1)$ and per-worker quantities $k = K/L$, $y = Y/L$: $$\boxed{y = f(k)}$$
3
Properties of $f$. Because $F$ is concave, $f$ inherits $f(0)=0$, $f'(k)>0$, $f''(k)<0$. The Inada conditions carry over: $f'(0)=+\infty$ and $f'(\infty)=0$, which guarantee a unique interior steady state.
Equation

Capital Accumulation

Output is split between consumption and investment. In a closed economy without government, savings equal investment:

$$Y = C + I, \qquad I = S = sY$$

Capital evolves as gross investment minus depreciation:

$$\frac{dK}{dt} = sF(K,L) - \delta K$$
$s$ — saving rate (= net investment rate)
$\delta$ — capital depreciation rate
$n$ — population growth rate ($\dot{L}/L = n$)
1
Goods market. All output is either consumed or invested: $Y = C + I$.
2
Saving = Investment. In a closed economy without government, $S = I$. Households save a fixed fraction $s$ of income: $S = sY$. Therefore $I = sY = sF(K,L)$.
3
Gross vs. net investment. $I$ is gross investment (total new capital installed). But capital also depreciates at rate $\delta$: each period, $\delta K$ units wear out. Net investment = gross investment $-$ depreciation: $$\frac{dK}{dt} = I - \delta K = sF(K,L) - \delta K$$

Converting to per-worker terms (the key equation of the model):

$$\frac{dk}{dt} = sf(k) - (\delta + n)k$$
1
Differentiate $k = K/L$ with respect to time using the quotient rule: $$\dot{k} = \frac{\dot{K}}{L} - \frac{K\,\dot{L}}{L^2} = \frac{\dot{K}}{L} - k\,\frac{\dot{L}}{L}$$
2
Substitute known expressions. We have $\dot{K} = sF(K,L) - \delta K$ and $\dot{L}/L = n$: $$\dot{k} = \frac{sF(K,L) - \delta K}{L} - nk$$
3
Simplify. $F(K,L)/L = f(k)$ (intensive form) and $K/L = k$: $$\dot{k} = sf(k) - \delta k - nk$$
4
Collect terms: $$\boxed{\dot{k} = sf(k) - (\delta + n)k}$$

$sf(k)$ is investment per worker. $(\delta+n)k$ is break-even investment: $\delta k$ replaces depreciated capital, $nk$ equips new workers so $k$ stays constant.

Intuition: $k$ grows if and only if investment per worker exceeds break-even investment. The $nk$ term appears because a growing population dilutes capital — you need to give new workers capital just to keep $k$ constant.
Graph

The Solow Diagram

$sf(k)$ (concave, green) intersects $(\delta+n)k$ (dashed) at the unique steady state $k^*$. Below $k^*$: investment exceeds break-even → $k$ rises. Above $k^*$: the reverse. Arrows show global convergence.
✎ Exercise 1
The Cobb-Douglas Case

In the case where $Y = K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}$:

1.Show that when production factors are paid at their marginal returns, $\alpha$ is the share of capital income in GDP, and $1-\alpha$ the share of labour income.
2.Show that $f(k) = k^\alpha$ where $k = K/L$.
3.What is the value of $k^*$ at which $dk/dt = 0$?
4.For this $k^*$, what is the growth rate of GDP per capita?
5.For this $k^*$, what is the growth rate of GDP?
6.Show that $s$ can be expressed in terms of $k^*$, $n$ and $\delta$.
7.Show that the growth rate of $k$ can be expressed in terms of $k/k^*$.
8.What is the growth rate of GDP per capita (out of steady state)?
9.What is the growth rate of GDP (out of steady state)?
1 — Capital and labour shares

Compute the marginal product of capital: $F_K = \alpha K^{\alpha-1}L^{1-\alpha} = \alpha\,\dfrac{Y}{K}$.

If capital is paid its marginal product, capital income $= F_K \cdot K = \alpha Y$. Its share in GDP is therefore $\alpha$.

Similarly, $F_L = (1-\alpha)K^\alpha L^{-\alpha} = (1-\alpha)\dfrac{Y}{L}$, so labour income $= F_L \cdot L = (1-\alpha)Y$ and labour’s share is $1-\alpha$.

Check: $\alpha + (1-\alpha) = 1$ — consistent with CRS (Euler’s theorem: $F_K K + F_L L = Y$). ✓

2 — Intensive form $f(k)$

Divide $Y = K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}$ by $L$: $$y = \frac{Y}{L} = \frac{K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}}{L} = \left(\frac{K}{L}\right)^\alpha L^{1-\alpha-1} \cdot L^0 = k^\alpha$$

So $f(k) = k^\alpha$. Note $f''(k) = \alpha(\alpha-1)k^{\alpha-2} < 0$ since $0 < \alpha < 1$ — confirms diminishing returns. ✓

3 — Steady-state capital $k^*$

At $dk/dt = 0$: $s\,k^{*\,\alpha} = (\delta+n)k^*$, i.e. $k^{*\,\alpha-1} = \dfrac{\delta+n}{s}$.

$$\boxed{k^* = \left(\frac{s}{\delta+n}\right)^{\!1/(1-\alpha)}}$$

As expected: higher $s$ or lower $n$, $\delta$ raises $k^*$.

4 — Growth rate of GDP per capita at $k^*$

At the steady state, $k$ is constant by definition. Since $y^* = k^{*\alpha}$, $y$ is also constant. The growth rate of GDP per capita is 0.

5 — Growth rate of GDP at $k^*$

$Y = yL$. With $\dot{y}/y = 0$ and $\dot{L}/L = n$, we get $\dot{Y}/Y = n$. Aggregate GDP grows at rate $n$ (extensive growth only).

6 — Saving rate in terms of $k^*$, $n$, $\delta$

From the steady-state condition $s k^{*\alpha} = (\delta+n)k^*$:

$$\boxed{s = (\delta+n)\,k^{*\,1-\alpha}}$$
7 — Growth rate of $k$ as a function of $k/k^*$

The growth rate of $k$ is $\hat{k} \equiv \dot{k}/k = sk^{\alpha-1} - (\delta+n)$.

Substitute $s = (\delta+n)k^{*\,1-\alpha}$:

$$\hat{k} = (\delta+n)\,k^{*\,1-\alpha}\,k^{\alpha-1} - (\delta+n) = (\delta+n)\left[\left(\frac{k^*}{k}\right)^{\!1-\alpha} - 1\right]$$

Interpretation: when $k < k^*$, the bracket is positive $\Rightarrow$ $k$ grows. When $k = k^*$, bracket $= 0$ $\Rightarrow$ steady state. The further $k$ is below $k^*$, the faster the growth.

8 — Growth rate of GDP per capita (out of steady state)

$y = k^\alpha \Rightarrow \hat{y} = \dot{y}/y = \alpha\,\hat{k}$: $$\boxed{\hat{y} = \alpha(\delta+n)\left[\left(\frac{k^*}{k}\right)^{\!1-\alpha} - 1\right]}$$

9 — Growth rate of GDP (out of steady state)

$Y = yL \Rightarrow g_Y = \hat{y} + n$: $$\boxed{g_Y = \alpha(\delta+n)\left[\left(\frac{k^*}{k}\right)^{\!1-\alpha} - 1\right] + n}$$

Extension

Adding Total Factor Productivity

$$Y = (AL)^{1-\alpha}K^\alpha$$

$A$ is Total Factor Productivity (TFP), growing at exogenous rate $g$:

$$\frac{\dot{A}}{A} = g$$

Redefine $k = K/(AL)$ (capital per efficient worker). The law of motion becomes:

$$\frac{dk}{dt} = sf(k) - (\delta + n + g)k$$
1
Differentiate $k = K/(AL)$: $$\dot{k} = \frac{\dot{K}}{AL} - k\,\frac{d(AL)/dt}{AL} = \frac{\dot{K}}{AL} - k\left(\frac{\dot{A}}{A}+\frac{\dot{L}}{L}\right)$$
2
Substitute $\dot{A}/A = g$, $\dot{L}/L = n$, and $\dot{K} = sY - \delta K$: $$\dot{k} = \frac{sY - \delta K}{AL} - (n+g)k = sf(k) - \delta k - (n+g)k$$
3
$$\boxed{\dot{k} = sf(k) - (\delta+n+g)k}$$

The extra $gk$ term captures the technology dilution effect: TFP growth makes each unit of effective labour more productive, raising the effective workforce $AL$ and diluting capital per efficient worker.

At the steady state of this extended model, GDP per capita grows at rate $g$ (TFP growth), and aggregate GDP grows at $n+g$. This resolves the original Solow model’s limitation: it now generates sustained per-capita growth.

Answer the same questions as in Exercise 1 in this specific case, with $k = K/(AL)$ and $(\delta+n+g)$ replacing $(\delta+n)$.

2 — Intensive form

Divide $Y = (AL)^{1-\alpha}K^\alpha$ by $AL$:

At $dk/dt = 0$: $sk^{*\,\alpha} = (\delta+n+g)k^*$, so:

$$k^* = \left(\frac{s}{\delta+n+g}\right)^{\!1/(1-\alpha)}$$
4 & 5 — Growth rates at steady state

At $k^*$, $k = K/(AL)$ is constant. Since $A$ grows at $g$, $K/L$ grows at $g$ too, so $y = Y/L$ grows at $g$. Aggregate GDP $Y = y \cdot L$ grows at $n+g$.

→ Growth rate of GDP per capita: $\boldsymbol{g}$. Growth rate of GDP: $\boldsymbol{n+g}$.

6 — Saving rate in terms of $k^*$
$$s = (\delta+n+g)\,k^{*\,1-\alpha}$$
7 — Growth rate of $k$ in terms of $k/k^*$
$$\hat{k} = (\delta+n+g)\left[\left(\frac{k^*}{k}\right)^{\!1-\alpha} - 1\right]$$
8 & 9 — Growth rates out of steady state

Growth of GDP per capita: $g_y = g + \alpha(\delta+n+g)\!\left[\left(\dfrac{k^*}{k}\right)^{1-\alpha}-1\right]$

Growth of GDP: $g_Y = g_y + n$

Golden Rule 1/2

The Saving Rate that Maximises Consumption

Is there a saving rate $s$ that maximises consumption per efficient worker at the steady state?

Step 1 — Express $s$ in terms of $k^*$

$$s = (\delta+n+g)\,k^{*\,1-\alpha}$$

Step 2 — Write consumption per efficient worker

$$c = (1-s)\,k^{*\,\alpha} = k^{*\,\alpha} - (\delta+n+g)\,k^*$$

Step 3 — Maximise over $k^*$

$$\frac{dc}{dk^*} = \alpha k^{*\,\alpha-1} - (\delta+n+g) = 0 \implies k^{**} = \left(\frac{\alpha}{\delta+n+g}\right)^{\!1/(1-\alpha)}$$

The saving rate corresponding to $k^{**}$ is $s = \alpha$:

$$k^{**} = \left(\frac{\alpha}{\delta+n+g}\right)^{\!1/(1-\alpha)} \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad s = \alpha$$
The Golden Rule: When the saving rate equals the share of capital income in GDP ($s = \alpha$), the steady state maximises consumption per efficient worker. This is called the golden rule saving rate.
✎ Golden Rule 2/2
A Higher Saving Rate Is Not Necessarily Better

Consider two cases:

1. An economy where $s > \alpha$. What would happen to per capita consumption of all generations if one could enforce $s = \alpha$?
2. What if, on the opposite, $s < \alpha$?
1 — Case $s > \alpha$ (over-saving)

If $s > \alpha$, the economy’s steady state $k^*$ is above the golden rule $k^{**}$. The economy is saving too much: it sacrifices current consumption to accumulate capital beyond what maximises long-run welfare.

Reducing $s$ to $\alpha$ has two effects that both work in the same direction:

(i) Immediate gain: less saving $\Rightarrow$ more consumption right now. Current generations benefit immediately.

(ii) Long-run gain: the new steady state has higher $c^*$. Future generations also benefit.

Since every generation gains, the move to the golden rule is Pareto improving. The economy was dynamically inefficient (over-accumulating capital).

2 — Case $s < \alpha$ (under-saving)

If $s < \alpha$, the economy is below the golden rule. Raising $s$ to $\alpha$ would increase the long-run steady-state consumption — but at a cost:

Short-run sacrifice: higher saving means lower consumption today. Current and near-future generations must sacrifice.

Long-run gain: capital accumulates more, and eventually the new (higher) $k^{**}$ is reached, raising $c^*$ for all future generations.

This creates a trade-off between generations: it is not Pareto improving. Whether to move to the golden rule involves a value judgement about how to weigh current vs. future generations.
