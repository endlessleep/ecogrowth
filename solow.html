Chapter 1 — The Neoclassical Growth Model

The Solow Model

Capital accumulation, steady states, and long-run economic growth.

Core Foundations

Strong Assumptions

The Solow model relies on three fundamental assumptions to study how an economy accumulates capital over time:

1
Constant Returns to Scale
Definition: If we multiply all inputs (capital $K$ and labour $L$) by a positive factor $\lambda$, total output $Y$ is multiplied by that exact same factor:
$F(\lambda K, \lambda L) = \lambda F(K,L) \qquad \forall \lambda > 0$
Why it matters: It lets us divide everything by labour $L$ (setting $\lambda = 1/L$) so we can write the entire economy in per-worker terms: $y = f(k)$.
2
Decreasing Marginal Returns of Inputs
★ Most Important
Definition: Adding one more unit of capital (holding labour fixed) always increases output, but by smaller and smaller amounts:
$F_K > 0 \quad \text{(positive return)} \qquad \text{and} \qquad F_{KK} < 0 \quad \text{(diminishing return)}$
Why it is the most important: This is the main reason why economic growth eventually slows down. When capital is scarce, each new machine adds a lot of output. As capital accumulates, each additional machine adds less and less, until new investment can only compensate for worn-out capital and population dilution.
Inada conditions: At $K \to 0$, marginal productivity is infinite ($\lim_{K\to 0} F_K = \infty$). As $K \to \infty$, it drops to zero ($\lim_{K\to \infty} F_K = 0$). This guarantees that a steady state always exists and is unique.
3
Constant and Exogenous Saving Rate
Definition: The economy saves a fixed fraction $s \in (0,1)$ of total income $Y$ each period:
$S = sY \qquad \text{so} \qquad I = sY$
Why it is an assumption: In the real world, households decide how much to save depending on interest rates, wages, and future plans. Here, $s$ is taken as given (exogenous) to keep the mechanics straightforward.
Key Takeaways

Main Messages

Three key conclusions to remember for exams and revision:

→
1. Each economy converges to a long-term steady-state path
Because of diminishing returns of capital, the extra output gained from new machines gets smaller as capital accumulates. Eventually, new investment exactly balances capital depreciation and population dilution ($dk/dt = 0$). No matter where the economy starts, it naturally moves toward this stable equilibrium ($k^*$).
🌎
2. Steady states can be different from one country to another
The long-run level of capital and income depends directly on national parameters: the saving rate ($s$), population growth ($n$), and depreciation ($\delta$). A country that saves more or has lower population growth reaches a higher steady state ($k^*$), which explains persistent differences in GDP per capita across the world.
⚡
3. The further away an economy is from its steady state, the higher its growth rate
When capital is very low compared to its steady state ($k \ll k^*$), the marginal product of capital is very high, so investment produces large gains and growth is fast. As the economy gets closer to $k^*$, growth gradually slows down. This property is known as conditional convergence.
Equation

Production Function

Total output (GDP) depends on physical capital and labour:

$$Y = F(K, L)$$
$Y$ — GDP (total production)
$K$ — stock of physical capital
$L$ — labour force
$F$ — production technology

Constant returns to scale

$$F(\lambda K, \lambda L) = \lambda F(K, L) \qquad \forall \lambda > 0$$

Positive and decreasing marginal returns

$$F_K > 0,\quad F_{KK} < 0,\quad F_L > 0,\quad F_{LL} < 0$$
$$\lim_{K\to 0}F_K = \infty, \quad \lim_{K\to\infty}F_K = 0, \quad \lim_{L\to 0}F_L = \infty, \quad \lim_{L\to\infty}F_L = 0$$
1
Use constant returns to scale: set $\lambda = \dfrac{1}{L}$. $$F\!\left(\frac{K}{L},\; \frac{L}{L}\right) = \frac{1}{L}\,F(K,L) \implies F\!\left(\frac{K}{L},\,1\right) = \frac{Y}{L}$$
2
Define per-worker variables:

Capital per worker: $k = \dfrac{K}{L}$  |  Output per worker: $y = \dfrac{Y}{L}$

Define the function $f(k) \equiv F(k, 1)$. We directly obtain:

$$\boxed{y = f(k)}$$
3
Properties of $f(k)$: It is concave with $f(0)=0$, $f'(k)>0$ (positive marginal product), and $f''(k)<0$ (diminishing marginal returns).
Equation

Capital Accumulation

Total output is either consumed or invested. In a closed economy with no government, savings equal investment:

$$Y = C + I, \qquad I = S = sY$$

Gross investment is $I = sF(K,L)$. Capital wears out at rate $\delta$, so net investment is:

$$\frac{dK}{dt} = sF(K,L) - \delta K$$
$s$ — saving rate
$\delta$ — capital depreciation rate
$n$ — population growth rate ($\dot{L}/L = n$)
$k = K/L$ — capital per worker
1
Goods market equilibrium: All output produced must be purchased for consumption $C$ or gross investment $I$: $$Y = C + I$$
2
Saving equals investment: In a closed economy without a public sector, national saving equals investment: $S = I$. Since households save a fraction $s$ of total income, $I = sY = sF(K,L)$.
3
Net change in capital stock: Capital changes according to gross investment minus depreciation $\delta K$: $$\boxed{\frac{dK}{dt} = I - \delta K = sF(K,L) - \delta K}$$

In per-worker terms, the fundamental law of motion is:

$$\frac{dk}{dt} = sf(k) - (\delta + n)k$$
1
Differentiate $k = \dfrac{K}{L}$ with respect to time $t$: $$\frac{dk}{dt} = \frac{\frac{dK}{dt} \cdot L - K \cdot \frac{dL}{dt}}{L^2} = \frac{1}{L}\frac{dK}{dt} - \frac{K}{L}\frac{\frac{dL}{dt}}{L}$$
2
Substitute the growth rates: We know $\dfrac{dL/dt}{L} = n$ and $\dfrac{dK}{dt} = sF(K,L) - \delta K$: $$\frac{dk}{dt} = \frac{sF(K,L) - \delta K}{L} - n k$$
3
Simplify using $F(K,L)/L = f(k)$ and $K/L = k$: $$\frac{dk}{dt} = s f(k) - \delta k - n k$$
4
Final equation: $$\boxed{\frac{dk}{dt} = sf(k) - (\delta + n)k}$$

• $sf(k)$ is actual investment per worker.
• $(\delta+n)k$ is break-even investment: the amount needed to replace worn-out machines ($\delta k$) and equip new workers ($nk$).

Graph

The Solow Diagram

The Solow diagram. The concave curve $sf(k)$ intersects the straight line $(\delta+n)k$ at the steady state $k^*$. When $k < k^*$, investment is greater than break-even needs, so $k$ rises. When $k > k^*$, investment is not enough, so $k$ falls.
✎ Exercise 1
The Cobb-Douglas Case

Consider the Cobb-Douglas production function: $Y = K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}$ (with $0 < \alpha < 1$).

1.Show that when production factors are paid at their marginal returns, $\alpha$ is the share of capital income in GDP, and $1-\alpha$ the share of labour income.
2.Show that $f(k) = k^\alpha$ where $k = K/L$.
3.What is the value of $k^*$ at which $dk/dt = 0$?
4.For this $k^*$, what is the growth rate of GDP per capita?
5.For this $k^*$, what is the growth rate of GDP?
6.Show that $s$ can be expressed in terms of $k^*$, $n$ and $\delta$.
7.Show that the growth rate of $k$ can be expressed in terms of $k/k^*$.
8.What is the growth rate of GDP per capita (out of steady state)?
9.What is the growth rate of GDP (out of steady state)?
Question 1 — Capital and labour income shares

Compute the marginal product of capital:

$$F_K = \frac{\partial Y}{\partial K} = \alpha K^{\alpha-1}L^{1-\alpha} = \alpha \frac{K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}}{K} = \alpha \frac{Y}{K}$$

If capital is paid its marginal product, total capital earnings are $F_K \cdot K = \alpha Y$. The share of capital in GDP is:

$$\frac{\text{Capital Income}}{Y} = \frac{\alpha Y}{Y} = \alpha$$

Similarly for labour: $F_L = (1-\alpha)\dfrac{Y}{L}$, so labour earnings are $F_L \cdot L = (1-\alpha)Y$, giving a labour share of $1-\alpha$.

Question 2 — Intensive form $f(k)$

Divide total output $Y$ by labour $L$:

$$y = \frac{Y}{L} = \frac{K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}}{L} = \frac{K^\alpha}{L^\alpha} = \left(\frac{K}{L}\right)^\alpha = k^\alpha$$

So $f(k) = k^\alpha$.

Question 3 — Steady-state capital $k^*$

Set $dk/dt = 0$ in the law of motion:

$$s f(k^*) = (\delta + n)k^* \implies s\,k^{*\alpha} = (\delta + n)k^*$$

Divide both sides by $k^*$ (since $k^* > 0$):

$$s\,k^{*\alpha-1} = \delta + n \implies k^{*-(1-\alpha)} = \frac{\delta+n}{s} \implies k^{*\,1-\alpha} = \frac{s}{\delta+n}$$ $$\boxed{k^* = \left(\frac{s}{\delta+n}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}}$$
Question 4 — Growth rate of GDP per capita at $k^*$

At the steady state, $k = k^*$ is constant. Output per worker is $y^* = (k^*)^\alpha$, which is also constant.

Therefore, the growth rate of GDP per capita is 0.

Question 5 — Growth rate of GDP at $k^*$

Total GDP is $Y = y \cdot L$. Since $y$ is constant and population grows at rate $n$ ($\dot{L}/L = n$), total GDP grows at the rate $n$.

Question 6 — Saving rate $s$ in terms of $k^*$, $n$, and $\delta$

From the steady-state equation $s\,k^{*\alpha} = (\delta+n)k^*$, isolate $s$:

$$\boxed{s = (\delta+n)\,k^{*\,1-\alpha}}$$
Question 7 — Growth rate of $k$ in terms of $k/k^*$

The growth rate of $k$ is $\dfrac{\dot{k}}{k} = \dfrac{s f(k) - (\delta+n)k}{k} = s k^{\alpha-1} - (\delta+n)$.

Replace $s$ by $(\delta+n)k^{*\,1-\alpha}$:

$$\frac{\dot{k}}{k} = (\delta+n)k^{*\,1-\alpha}\,k^{\alpha-1} - (\delta+n) = (\delta+n)\left[\frac{k^{*\,1-\alpha}}{k^{1-\alpha}} - 1\right]$$ $$\boxed{\frac{\dot{k}}{k} = (\delta+n)\left[\left(\frac{k^*}{k}\right)^{\!1-\alpha} - 1\right]}$$
Question 8 — Growth rate of GDP per capita (out of steady state)

Since $y = k^\alpha$, taking logs and differentiating with respect to time gives $\dfrac{\dot{y}}{y} = \alpha\,\dfrac{\dot{k}}{k}$.

$$\boxed{\frac{\dot{y}}{y} = \alpha(\delta+n)\left[\left(\frac{k^*}{k}\right)^{\!1-\alpha} - 1\right]}$$
Question 9 — Growth rate of GDP (out of steady state)

Since $Y = y \cdot L$, the growth rate of total GDP is the growth rate of GDP per capita plus population growth:

$$\boxed{\frac{\dot{Y}}{Y} = \frac{\dot{y}}{y} + n = \alpha(\delta+n)\left[\left(\frac{k^*}{k}\right)^{\!1-\alpha} - 1\right] + n}$$
Extension

Adding Total Factor Productivity

In the baseline model, GDP per capita stops growing in the long run. To explain sustained long-term growth, we introduce technological progress:

$$Y = (AL)^{1-\alpha}K^\alpha$$

where $A$ represents Total Factor Productivity (technology level), which grows at the exogenous rate $g$:

$$\frac{dA/dt}{A} = g$$

We now define capital per efficient worker as $k = \dfrac{K}{AL}$. The new law of motion is:

$$\frac{dk}{dt} = sf(k) - (\delta + n + g)k$$
1
Differentiate $k = \dfrac{K}{AL}$ with respect to time: $$\frac{dk}{dt} = \frac{\dot{K}(AL) - K \frac{d(AL)}{dt}}{(AL)^2} = \frac{\dot{K}}{AL} - k\,\frac{\frac{d(AL)}{dt}}{AL}$$
2
Growth of effective labour $AL$: Since $A$ grows at rate $g$ and $L$ grows at rate $n$, effective labour grows at rate $n+g$: $$\frac{\frac{d(AL)}{dt}}{AL} = \frac{\dot{A}}{A} + \frac{\dot{L}}{L} = g + n$$
3
Substitute $\dot{K} = sY - \delta K$: $$\frac{dk}{dt} = \frac{sY - \delta K}{AL} - (n+g)k = s\,\frac{Y}{AL} - \delta k - (n+g)k$$

Since $\dfrac{Y}{AL} = f(k)$:

$$\boxed{\frac{dk}{dt} = sf(k) - (\delta + n + g)k}$$

The additional term $gk$ reflects the fact that technological progress expands the effective labour force, diluting capital per efficient worker.

✎ Exercise 2
The Cobb-Douglas Case with TFP

Answer the same questions as in Exercise 1 in this specific case, where $k = K/(AL)$ and break-even depreciation includes $g$ ($(\delta+n+g)$ instead of $(\delta+n)$).

Intensive form $f(k)$

Divide $Y = (AL)^{1-\alpha}K^\alpha$ by $AL$:

$$\tilde{y} = \frac{Y}{AL} = \frac{K^\alpha}{(AL)^\alpha} = \left(\frac{K}{AL}\right)^\alpha = k^\alpha \implies f(k) = k^\alpha$$
Steady-state capital $k^*$

Set $dk/dt = 0 \implies s\,k^{*\alpha} = (\delta+n+g)k^*$. Solving for $k^*$ gives:

$$\boxed{k^* = \left(\frac{s}{\delta+n+g}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}}$$
Growth rates at the steady state

• Capital per efficient worker $k = K/(AL)$ is constant (growth rate = 0).

• GDP per capita $y = Y/L = A \cdot \tilde{y} = A \cdot f(k^*)$. Since $f(k^*)$ is constant, $y$ grows at the rate of technology: $g$.

• Total GDP $Y = y \cdot L$. Since $y$ grows at $g$ and $L$ grows at $n$, total GDP grows at: $n + g$.

Saving rate $s$
$$\boxed{s = (\delta+n+g)\,k^{*\,1-\alpha}}$$
Growth rate of $k$
$$\boxed{\frac{\dot{k}}{k} = (\delta+n+g)\left[\left(\frac{k^*}{k}\right)^{\!1-\alpha} - 1\right]}$$
Growth rates out of steady state

• Growth rate of GDP per capita: $\dfrac{\dot{y}}{y} = g + \alpha(\delta+n+g)\left[\left(\dfrac{k^*}{k}\right)^{\!1-\alpha} - 1\right]$

• Growth rate of total GDP: $\dfrac{\dot{Y}}{Y} = \dfrac{\dot{y}}{y} + n$

Golden Rule 1/2

The Saving Rate that Maximises Consumption

Question: Is there a saving rate $s$ that maximises consumption per efficient worker at the steady state?

Step 1 — Express $s$ in terms of $k^*$

$$s = (\delta+n+g)\,k^{*\,1-\alpha}$$

Step 2 — Write steady-state consumption per efficient worker

$$c = (1-s)k^{*\alpha} = \left(1 - (\delta+n+g)k^{*\,1-\alpha}\right)k^{*\alpha} = k^{*\alpha} - (\delta+n+g)k^*$$

Step 3 — Maximise $c$ with respect to $k^*$

Take the derivative with respect to $k^*$ and set it to 0:

$$\frac{dc}{dk^*} = \alpha k^{*\alpha-1} - (\delta+n+g) = 0 \implies k^{*\alpha-1} = \frac{\delta+n+g}{\alpha}$$
$$k^{**} = \left(\frac{\alpha}{\delta+n+g}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{1-\alpha}} \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad s = \alpha$$
The Golden Rule: When the saving rate equals the share of capital income in GDP ($s = \alpha$), steady-state consumption per efficient worker reaches its maximum.
✎ Golden Rule 2/2
A Higher Saving Rate Is Not Always Better

Consider an economy where the golden rule is not respected:

1. If $s > \alpha$ (over-saving): What would happen to per capita consumption across all generations if the government enforced the golden rule saving rate $s = \alpha$?
2. If $s < \alpha$ (under-saving): What happens if the government raises the saving rate to $s = \alpha$?
Case 1 — $s > \alpha$ (Dynamic Inefficiency)

When $s > \alpha$, the economy is saving too much. Its capital stock is higher than the golden rule level ($k^* > k^{**}$), meaning too much output is spent just maintaining the capital stock rather than being consumed.

If the saving rate is lowered to $s = \alpha$:

• Immediately: Households save less, so they consume more output right away.

• In the long run: Capital settles at $k^{**}$, where steady-state consumption is at its highest.

Result: Every generation (present and future) consumes more. This is a Pareto improvement.

Case 2 — $s < \alpha$ (Dynamic Efficiency)

When $s < \alpha$, the economy has less capital than the golden rule level ($k^* < k^{**}$).

If the saving rate is increased to $s = \alpha$:

• In the short run: Households must save more today, which lowers their current consumption.

• In the long run: More capital accumulates, eventually leading to higher steady-state consumption for future generations.

Result: Current generations make a sacrifice for the benefit of future generations. This is not a Pareto improvement; it represents an intergenerational trade-off.
