Chapter 1 — The Neoclassical Growth Model

The Solow Model

Capital accumulation, steady states, and long-run economic growth.

Core Foundations

Strong Assumptions

The Solow model uses three key assumptions to analyze long-term capital accumulation:

1
Constant Returns to Scale
Definition: Multiplying all inputs (capital $K$ and labour $L$) by any positive number $\lambda$ multiplies total production $Y$ by that same number:
$$F(\lambda K, \lambda L) = \lambda F(K, L) \qquad \forall \lambda > 0$$
Why it matters: By choosing $\lambda = 1/L$, we can write the entire production function in per-worker terms: $y = f(k)$.
2
Decreasing Marginal Returns of Inputs
★ Most Important
Definition: Adding an additional unit of capital (holding labour fixed) increases output, but by a smaller amount each time:
$$F_K > 0 \quad \text{(positive return)} \qquad \text{and} \qquad F_{KK} < 0 \quad \text{(diminishing return)}$$
Why it is the most important: This is why growth slows down. When capital is low, each new unit brings high gains. As capital grows, each extra unit adds less and less, eventually balancing out with depreciation and population growth.
Inada conditions: When $K \to 0$, marginal return is infinite ($\lim_{K\to 0} F_K = \infty$). When $K \to \infty$, it approaches zero ($\lim_{K\to\infty} F_K = 0$). This guarantees that a unique steady state $k^*$ exists.
3
Constant and Exogenous Saving Rate
Definition: Households save a fixed share $s \in (0,1)$ of total income $Y$:
$$S = sY \qquad \implies \qquad I = sY$$
Why it is assumed: In reality, people adjust how much they save according to interest rates and future needs. Taking $s$ as a constant parameter simplifies the model while keeping the main mechanisms clear.
Key Takeaways

Main Messages

Three essential conclusions from the Solow framework:

→
1. Each economy converges to a long-term steady-state path
Due to decreasing marginal returns, new investment eventually balances out the capital lost to depreciation and population dilution ($dk/dt = 0$). Regardless of its starting point, the economy naturally heads toward this stable steady state ($k^*$).
🌍
2. Steady states can be different from one country to another
Long-run capital and income depend on local parameters: saving rate ($s$), population growth ($n$), and depreciation ($\delta$). Countries with higher saving rates or lower population growth achieve a higher $k^*$, explaining persistent cross-country income differences.
⚡
3. The further away an economy is from its steady state, the higher its growth rate
When capital is very low compared to its steady state ($k \ll k^*$), the return on capital is high, making investment very productive and growth fast. As $k$ nears $k^*$, growth slows down. This property is called conditional convergence.
Equation

Production Function

Total output (GDP) is generated by physical capital and labour:

$$Y = F(K, L)$$
$Y$ — GDP (total production)
$K$ — stock of physical capital
$L$ — labour force
$F$ — production technology

Constant returns to scale

$$F(\lambda K, \lambda L) = \lambda F(K, L) \qquad \forall \lambda > 0$$

Positive and decreasing marginal returns

$$F_K > 0,\quad F_{KK} < 0,\quad F_L > 0,\quad F_{LL} < 0$$
$$\lim_{K\to 0}F_K = \infty, \quad \lim_{K\to\infty}F_K = 0, \quad \lim_{L\to 0}F_L = \infty, \quad \lim_{L\to\infty}F_L = 0$$
1
Apply constant returns to scale: set $\lambda = \dfrac{1}{L}$. $$F\!\left(\frac{K}{L},\; \frac{L}{L}\right) = \frac{1}{L}\,F(K,L) \implies F\!\left(\frac{K}{L},\,1\right) = \frac{Y}{L}$$
2
Define per-worker variables:

Capital per worker: $k = \dfrac{K}{L}$  |  Output per worker: $y = \dfrac{Y}{L}$

Define the function $f(k) \equiv F(k, 1)$. We directly obtain:

$$\boxed{y = f(k)}$$
3
Properties of $f(k)$: $f(0)=0$, $f'(k)>0$ (positive marginal product), and $f''(k) < 0$ (diminishing marginal returns).
Equation

Capital Accumulation

Total output is either consumed or invested. In a closed economy without a public sector, savings equal investment:

$$Y = C + I, \qquad I = S = sY$$

Gross investment is $I = sF(K,L)$. Because capital depreciates at rate $\delta$, net investment is:

$$\frac{dK}{dt} = sF(K,L) - \delta K$$
$s$ — saving rate
$\delta$ — capital depreciation rate
$n$ — population growth rate ($\frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt} = n$)
$k = K/L$ — capital per worker
1
Goods market equilibrium: All output produced is either consumed by households ($C$) or invested ($I$): $$Y = C + I$$
2
Saving equals investment: Households save a fraction $s$ of their income: $S = sY$. Since $S = I$, gross investment is $I = sY = sF(K,L)$.
3
Net investment: Net change in capital is gross investment minus depreciation ($\delta K$): $$\boxed{\frac{dK}{dt} = I - \delta K = sF(K,L) - \delta K}$$

In per-worker terms, the fundamental law of motion is:

$$\frac{dk}{dt} = sf(k) - (\delta + n)k$$
1
Differentiate $k = \dfrac{K}{L}$ with respect to time $t$: $$\frac{dk}{dt} = \frac{\frac{dK}{dt} \cdot L - K \cdot \frac{dL}{dt}}{L^2} = \frac{1}{L}\frac{dK}{dt} - \frac{K}{L}\frac{\frac{dL}{dt}}{L}$$
2
Substitute the growth rates: We know $\dfrac{dL/dt}{L} = n$ and $\dfrac{dK}{dt} = sF(K,L) - \delta K$: $$\frac{dk}{dt} = \frac{sF(K,L) - \delta K}{L} - n k$$
3
Simplify using $F(K,L)/L = f(k)$ and $K/L = k$: $$\frac{dk}{dt} = s f(k) - \delta k - n k$$
4
Collect terms: $$\boxed{\frac{dk}{dt} = sf(k) - (\delta + n)k}$$

• $sf(k)$ is actual investment per worker.
• $(\delta+n)k$ is break-even investment: the amount needed to replace worn-out machines ($\delta k$) and equip new workers ($nk$).

Graph

The Solow Diagram

The Solow diagram. The concave curve $sf(k)$ meets the break-even line $(\delta+n)k$ at steady state $k^*$. Below $k^*$, investment exceeds break-even requirements, so capital grows. Above $k^*$, capital depreciates faster than new investment, so capital falls.

Question 1 — Capital and Labour Income Shares in GDP

When factors of production are paid their marginal products:

$$r = \frac{\partial Y}{\partial K} \qquad \text{and} \qquad w = \frac{\partial Y}{\partial L}$$

where $r$ is the real return to capital and $w$ is the real wage rate.

Capital income share: Differentiate $Y = K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}$ with respect to $K$:

$$r = \frac{\partial Y}{\partial K} = \alpha K^{\alpha-1}L^{1-\alpha}$$

Total capital income earned by all capital owners is $rK$:

$$rK = \alpha K^{\alpha-1}L^{1-\alpha} \cdot K = \alpha K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha} = \alpha Y$$

Divide by total GDP $Y$ to get capital's share in output:

$$\boxed{\frac{rK}{Y} = \alpha}$$

Labour income share: Differentiate $Y = K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}$ with respect to $L$:

$$w = \frac{\partial Y}{\partial L} = (1-\alpha)K^\alpha L^{-\alpha}$$

Total labour income earned by all workers is $wL$:

$$wL = (1-\alpha)K^\alpha L^{-\alpha} \cdot L = (1-\alpha)K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha} = (1-\alpha)Y$$

Divide by total GDP $Y$ to get labour's share in output:

$$\boxed{\frac{wL}{Y} = 1-\alpha}$$

Conclusion: $\alpha$ is the share of capital income in GDP, and $1-\alpha$ is the share of labour income in GDP.

Question 2 — Intensive Form: $f(k) = k^\alpha$

Output per worker is defined as $y = \dfrac{Y}{L}$. Substitute the production function:

$$y = \frac{K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}}{L} = K^\alpha L^{-\alpha} = \left(\frac{K}{L}\right)^\alpha$$

Since capital per worker is defined as $k = \dfrac{K}{L}$, we obtain:

$$\boxed{y = f(k) = k^\alpha}$$

Interpretation: Output per worker depends solely on capital per worker. Because $0 < \alpha < 1$, the function has $f'(k) = \alpha k^{\alpha-1} > 0$ and $f''(k) = \alpha(\alpha-1)k^{\alpha-2} < 0$, which mathematically confirms diminishing marginal returns to capital.

Question 3 — Steady-State Capital per Worker: $k^*$

The capital accumulation equation is:

$$\frac{dk}{dt} = s k^\alpha - (\delta + n)k$$

At the steady state, capital per worker is constant by definition, so $\boxed{\dfrac{dk}{dt} = 0}$:

$$s k^{*\alpha} - (\delta + n)k^* = 0 \implies s k^{*\alpha} = (\delta + n)k^*$$

Divide both sides by $k^{*\alpha}$ (or divide by $k^*$):

$$k^{*\,1-\alpha} = \frac{s}{\delta + n}$$

Raise both sides to the power $\dfrac{1}{1-\alpha}$:

$$\boxed{k^* = \left(\frac{s}{\delta + n}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}}$$

Interpretation: $k^*$ is the long-run equilibrium level of capital per worker where gross investment per worker ($s f(k^*)$) exactly compensates for capital depreciation ($\delta k^*$) and population dilution ($nk^*$).

Question 4 — Growth Rate of GDP per Capita at $k^*$

Output per worker depends on capital per worker: $y = k^\alpha$.

Step 1 — Full time derivative: Differentiate with respect to time using the chain rule:

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = \alpha k^{\alpha-1} \frac{dk}{dt}$$

Divide by $y = k^\alpha$ to obtain the general growth rate of GDP per capita:

$$\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{1}{k^\alpha} \cdot \alpha k^{\alpha-1} \frac{dk}{dt} = \alpha \frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt}$$
$$\boxed{\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} = \alpha \frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt}}$$

Step 2 — At the steady state ($k = k^*$): By definition, capital per worker is constant, so $\dfrac{dk}{dt} = 0$. Writing the full derivative explicitly:

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = \alpha (k^*)^{\alpha-1} \underbrace{\frac{dk}{dt}}_{=0} = 0$$

Therefore, the growth rate of GDP per capita at the steady state is:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} = \alpha \frac{1}{k^*} \underbrace{\frac{dk}{dt}}_{=0} = 0}$$

Interpretation: GDP per capita does not equal zero; rather, its growth rate is zero. Output per worker settles at a constant level: $y^* = (k^*)^\alpha$.

Question 5 — Growth Rate of Total GDP at $k^*$

Total GDP is output per worker times the number of workers: $Y = y \cdot L$.

Differentiate with respect to time using the product rule:

$$\frac{dY}{dt} = L \frac{dy}{dt} + y \frac{dL}{dt}$$

Divide the entire equation by $Y = yL$:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt} = \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} + \frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt}}$$

By assumption, population grows at rate $n$, so $\dfrac{1}{L}\dfrac{dL}{dt} = n$. At the steady state, substitute the steady-state result $\dfrac{1}{y}\dfrac{dy}{dt} = 0$ from Question 4:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt}}_{=0} + \frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt} = 0 + n = n}$$

Interpretation: While living standards per person are constant in the steady state, the total economy grows at rate $n$ because the labour force is expanding at rate $n$ (extensive growth only).

Question 6 — Expressing $s$ in Terms of $k^*$, $n$, and $\delta$

At the steady state, $\dfrac{dk}{dt} = 0 \implies s k^{*\alpha} = (\delta + n)k^*$.

Divide both sides by $k^{*\alpha}$:

$$\boxed{s = (\delta + n)k^{*\,1-\alpha}}$$

This expression allows us to replace $s$ directly in the growth equation below.

Question 7 — Growth Rate of $k$ in Terms of $k/k^*$

Start from the capital accumulation equation: $\dfrac{dk}{dt} = s k^\alpha - (\delta + n)k$.

Divide by $k$ to obtain the growth rate of capital per worker:

$$\frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt} = s k^{\alpha-1} - (\delta + n)$$

Substitute the steady-state saving rate $s = (\delta + n)k^{*\,1-\alpha}$ from Question 6:

$$\frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt} = (\delta + n)k^{*\,1-\alpha}k^{\alpha-1} - (\delta + n) = (\delta + n)\left[k^{*\,1-\alpha}k^{\alpha-1} - 1\right]$$

Notice that $k^{*\,1-\alpha}k^{\alpha-1} = \dfrac{k^{*\,1-\alpha}}{k^{1-\alpha}} = \left(\dfrac{k^*}{k}\right)^{1-\alpha}$. Thus:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt} = (\delta + n)\left[\left(\frac{k^*}{k}\right)^{\!1-\alpha} - 1\right]}$$

Equivalently, written in terms of $k/k^*$:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt} = (\delta + n)\left[\left(\frac{k}{k^*}\right)^{\!\alpha-1} - 1\right]}$$

Interpretation & Convergence Dynamics:

Since $\delta + n > 0$ and $1-\alpha > 0$ (because $0 < \alpha < 1$), the sign of the growth rate depends entirely on the bracket:

  • If $k < k^*$: then $\dfrac{k^*}{k} > 1 \implies \left(\dfrac{k^*}{k}\right)^{1-\alpha} > 1 \implies \boxed{\dfrac{1}{k}\dfrac{dk}{dt} > 0}$ (capital accumulates, $k \uparrow$).
  • If $k = k^*$: then $\dfrac{k^*}{k} = 1 \implies \boxed{\dfrac{1}{k}\dfrac{dk}{dt} = 0}$ ($k$ remains constant).
  • If $k > k^*$: then $\dfrac{k^*}{k} < 1 \implies \left(\dfrac{k^*}{k}\right)^{1-\alpha} < 1 \implies \boxed{\dfrac{1}{k}\dfrac{dk}{dt} < 0}$ (capital decumulates, $k \downarrow$).

This proves mathematically that the steady state $k^*$ is globally stable: from any initial level $k(0) > 0$, the economy converges toward $k^*$.

Question 8 — Growth Rate of GDP per Capita Outside the Steady State

From Question 4, we established that $\dfrac{1}{y}\dfrac{dy}{dt} = \alpha \dfrac{1}{k}\dfrac{dk}{dt}$.

Substitute the expression for $\dfrac{1}{k}\dfrac{dk}{dt}$ found in Question 7:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} = \alpha(\delta + n)\left[\left(\frac{k^*}{k}\right)^{\!1-\alpha} - 1\right]}$$

Interpretation:

  • If $k < k^*$: GDP per capita grows ($\dfrac{1}{y}\dfrac{dy}{dt} > 0$).
  • If $k = k^*$: GDP per capita is constant ($\dfrac{1}{y}\dfrac{dy}{dt} = 0$).
  • If $k > k^*$: GDP per capita falls ($\dfrac{1}{y}\dfrac{dy}{dt} < 0$).

As the economy approaches its steady state ($k \to k^*$), $\dfrac{k^*}{k} \to 1$, so $\dfrac{1}{y}\dfrac{dy}{dt} \to 0$. The growth rate of GDP per capita slows down continuously and converges toward zero.

Question 9 — Growth Rate of Total GDP Outside the Steady State

Recall from Question 5 that total GDP growth decomposes into two parts:

$$\text{GDP growth} = \text{GDP per capita growth} + \text{population growth}$$
$$\frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt} = \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} + \frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt}$$

Substitute $\dfrac{1}{L}\dfrac{dL}{dt} = n$ and the result from Question 8:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt} = \alpha(\delta + n)\left[\left(\frac{k^*}{k}\right)^{\!1-\alpha} - 1\right] + n}$$

Interpretation:

When an economy is developing ($k < k^*$), output per capita is expanding, so total GDP grows strictly faster than population growth ($\frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt} > n$).

As the economy gets closer to the steady state ($k \to k^*$), per-capita growth dries up ($\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} \to 0$), and total GDP growth converges toward population growth:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt} \longrightarrow n}$$
Core Takeaway — The Solow Convergence Mechanism:

• When $k < k^*$, capital per worker grows ($k \uparrow$).

• When $k > k^*$, capital per worker declines ($k \downarrow$).

• Therefore, the economy always converges to $k^*$. As it nears $k^*$, the growth rate of capital per worker drops to zero ($\frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt} \to 0$), the growth rate of GDP per capita drops to zero ($\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} \to 0$), and total GDP growth settles at the rate of population growth ($\frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt} \to n$).

Extension

Adding Total Factor Productivity

In the baseline model, GDP per capita stops growing in the long run. To explain sustained long-term growth, we introduce technological progress:

$$Y = (AL)^{1-\alpha}K^\alpha$$
$L$ — number of workers
$A$ — productivity of each worker
$AL$ — effective amount of labour available in the economy

where $A$ represents Total Factor Productivity (technology level), which grows at the exogenous rate $g$:

$$\frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt} = g$$

We now define capital per efficient worker as $k = \dfrac{K}{AL}$. The new law of motion is:

$$\frac{dk}{dt} = sf(k) - (\delta + n + g)k$$
1
Differentiate $k = \dfrac{K}{AL}$ with respect to time: $$\frac{dk}{dt} = \frac{\frac{dK}{dt}(AL) - K \frac{d(AL)}{dt}}{(AL)^2} = \frac{1}{AL}\frac{dK}{dt} - k\,\frac{\frac{d(AL)}{dt}}{AL}$$
2
Growth of effective labour $AL$: Since $A$ grows at rate $g$ and $L$ grows at rate $n$, effective labour grows at rate $n+g$: $$\frac{1}{AL}\frac{d(AL)}{dt} = \frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt} + \frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt} = g + n$$
3
Substitute $\frac{dK}{dt} = sY - \delta K$: $$\frac{dk}{dt} = \frac{sY - \delta K}{AL} - (n+g)k = s\,\frac{Y}{AL} - \delta k - (n+g)k$$

Since $\dfrac{Y}{AL} = f(k)$:

$$\boxed{\frac{dk}{dt} = sf(k) - (\delta + n + g)k}$$

The extra term $gk$ reflects technology dilution: rising worker productivity expands the effective labour supply, reducing capital per efficient worker.

Question 1 — Intensive Form: $f(k) = k^\alpha$ with $k = \dfrac{K}{AL}$

The production function with labour-augmenting technological progress is:

$$Y = (AL)^{1-\alpha} K^\alpha$$

Output per efficient worker is defined as $\tilde{y} = \dfrac{Y}{AL}$. Divide both sides by $AL$:

$$\tilde{y} = \frac{(AL)^{1-\alpha} K^\alpha}{AL} = \frac{K^\alpha}{(AL)^\alpha} = \left(\frac{K}{AL}\right)^\alpha$$

Since capital per efficient worker is defined as $k = \dfrac{K}{AL}$, we obtain:

$$\boxed{\tilde{y} = f(k) = k^\alpha}$$

Interpretation: In intensive form, production per efficient worker depends entirely on capital per efficient worker $k$. The technological level $A$ scales effective labour proportionally, which keeps returns to scale constant across the expanded inputs.

Question 2 — Steady-State Capital per Efficient Worker: $k^*$

The capital accumulation equation in intensive form is:

$$\frac{dk}{dt} = s k^\alpha - (\delta + n + g)k$$

At the steady state, capital per efficient worker is constant, so $\boxed{\dfrac{dk}{dt} = 0}$:

$$s k^{*\alpha} - (\delta + n + g)k^* = 0 \implies s k^{*\alpha} = (\delta + n + g)k^*$$

Divide both sides by $k^{*\alpha}$:

$$k^{*\,1-\alpha} = \frac{s}{\delta + n + g}$$

Raise both sides to the power $\dfrac{1}{1-\alpha}$:

$$\boxed{k^* = \left(\frac{s}{\delta + n + g}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}}$$

Interpretation: $k^*$ is the long-run equilibrium level of capital per efficient worker. Investment per efficient worker $s f(k^*)$ balances not only physical depreciation ($\delta k^*$) and population expansion ($n k^*$), but also technological dilution ($g k^*$).

Question 3 — Growth Rate of GDP per Capita at $k^*$

Output per worker (GDP per capita) is defined as $y = \dfrac{Y}{L}$. Using the production function $Y = (AL)^{1-\alpha}K^\alpha = (AL)\cdot k^\alpha$:

$$y = \frac{A L \cdot k^\alpha}{L} = A k^\alpha$$

Step 1 — Full time derivative: Differentiate $y = A k^\alpha$ with respect to time using the product rule and chain rule:

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{dA}{dt}k^\alpha + A\alpha k^{\alpha-1}\frac{dk}{dt}$$

Divide both sides by $y = A k^\alpha$ to obtain the general growth rate of GDP per capita:

$$\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{\frac{dA}{dt}k^\alpha}{A k^\alpha} + \frac{A\alpha k^{\alpha-1}\frac{dk}{dt}}{A k^\alpha} = \frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt} + \alpha \frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt}$$

Step 2 — At the steady state ($k = k^*$): By definition of the steady state, capital per efficient worker is constant, so $\dfrac{dk}{dt} = 0$. Writing the full derivative explicitly:

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{dA}{dt}(k^*)^\alpha + A\alpha(k^*)^{\alpha-1} \underbrace{\frac{dk}{dt}}_{=0} = \frac{dA}{dt}(k^*)^\alpha$$

Dividing by $y^* = A(k^*)^\alpha$ (or in terms of the growth rate formula):

$$\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt} + \alpha \frac{1}{k^*} \underbrace{\frac{dk}{dt}}_{=0} = \frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt}$$

Since technology grows at the exogenous rate $g$, i.e. $\dfrac{1}{A}\dfrac{dA}{dt} = g$:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} = g}$$

Interpretation: This is the central breakthrough of the Solow model with technological progress! Unlike the baseline model where living standards stagnate in the long run ($\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} = 0$), GDP per capita now grows continuously at rate $g$. Sustained growth in living standards is entirely driven by technological innovation.

Question 4 — Growth Rate of Total GDP at $k^*$

Total GDP is output per worker multiplied by total labour: $Y = y \cdot L$.

Differentiate with respect to time using the product rule:

$$\frac{dY}{dt} = \frac{dy}{dt}L + y\frac{dL}{dt}$$

Divide both sides by $Y = yL$ to obtain the general growth rate:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt} = \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} + \frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt}}$$

At the steady state, substitute the steady-state growth rate of GDP per capita ($\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} = g$) and the population growth rate ($\frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt} = n$):

$$\frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt}}_{=g} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt}}_{=n} = g + n$$
$$\boxed{\frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt} = g + n}$$

Interpretation: Total GDP grows at the combined rate of population growth and technological progress ($n + g$). Total output expands both because there are more workers ($n$) and because each worker becomes more productive over time ($g$).

Question 5 — Expressing $s$ in Terms of $k^*$, $n$, $g$, and $\delta$

At the steady state, $\dfrac{dk}{dt} = 0 \implies s k^{*\alpha} = (\delta + n + g)k^*$.

Divide both sides by $k^{*\alpha}$:

$$\boxed{s = (\delta + n + g)k^{*\,1-\alpha}}$$

This links the saving rate directly to the steady-state capital stock per efficient worker and the effective depreciation rate $(\delta + n + g)$.

Question 6 — Growth Rate of $k$ in Terms of $k/k^*$

Start from the capital accumulation equation: $\dfrac{dk}{dt} = s k^\alpha - (\delta + n + g)k$.

Divide by $k$ to obtain the proportional growth rate of capital per efficient worker:

$$\frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt} = s k^{\alpha-1} - (\delta + n + g)$$

Substitute $s = (\delta + n + g)k^{*\,1-\alpha}$ from Question 5:

$$\frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt} = (\delta + n + g)k^{*\,1-\alpha}k^{\alpha-1} - (\delta + n + g) = (\delta + n + g)\left[k^{*\,1-\alpha}k^{\alpha-1} - 1\right]$$

Since $k^{*\,1-\alpha}k^{\alpha-1} = \dfrac{k^{*\,1-\alpha}}{k^{1-\alpha}} = \left(\dfrac{k^*}{k}\right)^{1-\alpha}$, we obtain:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt} = (\delta + n + g)\left[\left(\frac{k^*}{k}\right)^{\!1-\alpha} - 1\right]}$$

Equivalently, written in terms of $k/k^*$:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt} = (\delta + n + g)\left[\left(\frac{k}{k^*}\right)^{\!\alpha-1} - 1\right]}$$

Interpretation & Convergence Dynamics:

Because $\delta + n + g > 0$ and $1-\alpha > 0$:

  • If $k < k^*$: then $\dfrac{k^*}{k} > 1 \implies \left(\dfrac{k^*}{k}\right)^{1-\alpha} > 1 \implies \boxed{\dfrac{1}{k}\dfrac{dk}{dt} > 0}$ (capital per efficient worker increases).
  • If $k = k^*$: then $\dfrac{k^*}{k} = 1 \implies \boxed{\dfrac{1}{k}\dfrac{dk}{dt} = 0}$ (steady-state equilibrium is maintained).
  • If $k > k^*$: then $\dfrac{k^*}{k} < 1 \implies \left(\dfrac{k^*}{k}\right)^{1-\alpha} < 1 \implies \boxed{\dfrac{1}{k}\dfrac{dk}{dt} < 0}$ (capital per efficient worker decreases).

This proves that $k^*$ is a stable equilibrium: whether an economy starts below or above $k^*$, capital per efficient worker converges to $k^*$.

Question 7 — Growth Rate of GDP per Capita Outside the Steady State

Recall from Question 3 (Step 1) that the full time derivative of $y = A k^\alpha$ gives the general growth rate of GDP per capita:

$$\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt} + \alpha \frac{1}{k}\frac{dk}{dt}$$

Outside the steady state, $\dfrac{dk}{dt} \neq 0$. Substitute the exogenous rate of technological progress $\dfrac{1}{A}\dfrac{dA}{dt} = g$ and the transitional growth rate of capital per efficient worker $\dfrac{1}{k}\dfrac{dk}{dt}$ from Question 6:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} = g + \alpha(\delta + n + g)\left[\left(\frac{k^*}{k}\right)^{\!1-\alpha} - 1\right]}$$

Interpretation:

The growth rate of GDP per capita is the sum of two components:

  • The permanent technology trend $g$: represents ongoing innovations and efficiency improvements.
  • The transitional capital dynamics: proportional to $\left[\left(\frac{k^*}{k}\right)^{1-\alpha} - 1\right]$.

When an economy is developing ($k < k^*$), it benefits from catch-up growth: it accumulates capital rapidly, so GDP per capita grows faster than $g$ ($\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} > g$). As $k \to k^*$, this transitional boost vanishes, and per-capita growth converges to $g$.

Question 8 — Growth Rate of Total GDP Outside the Steady State

From the relationship $Y = y \cdot L$, the growth rate of total GDP is:

$$\frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt} = \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt} + \frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt}$$

Substitute $\dfrac{1}{L}\dfrac{dL}{dt} = n$ and the growth rate of GDP per capita from Question 7:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt} = g + n + \alpha(\delta + n + g)\left[\left(\frac{k^*}{k}\right)^{\!1-\alpha} - 1\right]}$$

Interpretation:

If $k < k^*$, total GDP grows at a rate strictly higher than $n + g$. As the capital stock per efficient worker approaches its steady state ($k \to k^*$), the transitional term goes to zero, and total GDP growth converges smoothly to its balanced growth rate:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{Y}\frac{dY}{dt} \longrightarrow n + g}$$
Core Takeaway — The Solow Model with Technological Progress:

• Capital per efficient worker ($k$): converges to a stationary level $k^*$, where its growth rate is $0$.

• Living standards / GDP per capita ($y$): grows continuously at the rate of technological progress $g$ in the long run.

• Total economy / GDP ($Y$): expands at rate $n + g$ in the long run.

• Policy implication: Capital accumulation (higher saving rate $s$) creates a level effect (higher $k^*$ and higher $y^*$) and temporarily speeds up growth during transition, but only technological progress ($g$) can sustain long-term growth in per-capita income.

Golden Rule 1/2

The Saving Rate that Maximises Consumption

Question: Is there a saving rate $s$ that maximises consumption per efficient worker at the steady state?

Step 1 — Express $s$ in terms of $k^*$

$$s = (\delta+n+g)\,k^{*\,1-\alpha}$$

Step 2 — Write steady-state consumption per efficient worker

$$c = (1-s)k^{*\alpha} = \left(1 - (\delta+n+g)k^{*\,1-\alpha}\right)k^{*\alpha} = k^{*\alpha} - (\delta+n+g)k^*$$

Step 3 — Maximise $c$ with respect to $k^*$

Take the derivative with respect to $k^*$ and set it to 0:

$$\frac{dc}{dk^*} = \alpha k^{*\alpha-1} - (\delta+n+g) = 0 \implies k^{*\alpha-1} = \frac{\delta+n+g}{\alpha}$$
$$k^{**} = \left(\frac{\alpha}{\delta+n+g}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{1-\alpha}} \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad s = \alpha$$
The Golden Rule: When the saving rate equals the share of capital income in GDP ($s = \alpha$), steady-state consumption per efficient worker reaches its maximum.
✎ Golden Rule 2/2
A Higher Saving Rate Is Not Always Better

Consider an economy where the golden rule is not respected:

  • 1.
    If $s > \alpha$ (over-saving): What would happen to per capita consumption across all generations if the government enforced the golden rule saving rate $s = \alpha$?
  • 2.
    If $s < \alpha$ (under-saving): What happens if the government raises the saving rate to $s = \alpha$?
Case 1 — $s > \alpha$ (Dynamic Inefficiency)

When $s > \alpha$, the economy is saving too much. Its capital stock is higher than the golden rule level ($k^* > k^{**}$), meaning too much output is spent just maintaining the capital stock rather than being consumed.

If the saving rate is lowered to $s = \alpha$:

• Immediately: Households save less, so they consume more output right away.

• In the long run: Capital settles at $k^{**}$, where steady-state consumption is at its highest.

Result: Every generation (present and future) consumes more. This is a Pareto improvement.

Case 2 — $s < \alpha$ (Dynamic Efficiency)

When $s < \alpha$, the economy has less capital than the golden rule level ($k^* < k^{**}$).

If the saving rate is increased to $s = \alpha$:

• In the short run: Households must save more today, which lowers their current consumption.

• In the long run: More capital accumulates, eventually leading to higher steady-state consumption for future generations.

Result: Current generations make a sacrifice for the benefit of future generations. This is not a Pareto improvement; it represents an intergenerational trade-off.
